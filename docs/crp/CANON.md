**What this is.** The durable conceptual output of the figure-development sequence
(2026-08-24 to 2026-08-25), compressed into institutional knowledge. Building 40
concept slides forced every claim in the application to be stated as a picture, and
pictures cannot hide a vague argument. What survived that pressure is recorded here.

**Authority.** This file sits between `SPINE.md` (the locked strategic spine, one
paragraph) and the section drafts. Where it conflicts with an older document, this
file is current and the older document is marked; see section 6. It does not outrank
the solicitation, the SF424 instructions, or Olera's actual evidence. It is not
application text and no sentence here should be pasted into a section unedited.

**The artifact it came from.** `passes/payer-horizon-visuals-2026-08-24.html`, 43
pages, rendered to PDF alongside it. Sent to David, Marcia, TJ, and Qiping for review

**How to use it.** Before drafting any section of the Specific Aims, Research

and the section-3 entries the passage touches. Then check section 5 so an open
question does not enter the prose as a settled fact.

## 1. The one thing the exercise proved about the proposal itself

The application is a **causal chain, not a collection of sections**. Each claim
creates the next question, and the answer to that question is the next claim. When
the chain is stated in order, the reader never asks "why are you telling me this,"
because the previous sentence made them want to know. When a section is written
without knowing which question it answers and which question it creates, it reads as
inventory.

The chain, in the order the reader must meet it:

> Unmet need compounds into a vicious cycle (harm) → the cycle persists because the
> eldercare ecosystem is unnavigable and unaffordable (cause one) → navigation alone
> hits a caregiver-capacity bottleneck (cause two) → therefore navigation and new
> capacity together are what break the cycle (the synthesis) → so Olera is building
> both (the product) → but both must be paid for (the constraint) → here is who pays
> and for what (the business model) → competitors, academic work, and adoption
> hurdles do not close it (the gap) → three key innovations are why we can (the
> departure) → here is what would actually prove it (the research) → here is what two
> NIH awards already bought, and the one risk that is left (the warrant).

Every section of the written application is a station on that chain. The test for any
paragraph is the same as the test for any figure: **what question does the reader
arrive with, and what question do they leave with?**

## 2. Locked or high-confidence decisions

These are the working foundation. Change them only with Logan, and flag rather than
silently edit.

### 2.1 The problem narrative and the vicious cycle

- **The opening is harm, not market size.** Older adults' needs go unmet; unmet need
  produces preventable emergency care and hospitalization, caregiver breakdown, and
  premature institutionalization; each of those deepens the needs that went unmet.
  The cycle is the first figure and the first paragraph.
- **Name the cause in the same breath as the harm.** Care goes unestablished because
  it is **too expensive to afford and too confusing to find**. Stating only the
  consequences leaves the reader to guess the mechanism, and the guess is usually
  "these people did not try hard enough."
- **The delay and the cost inversion are part of the harm.** Families of modest means
  wait and decline until a hospitalization, at which point public programs step in at
  the most expensive possible moment and after the most damage. "Families wait, and
  public programs pay last and most" is the compressed form.
- **Needs are named concretely, never as "social determinants" or "unmet need"
  alone**: food, housing, income, transportation, healthcare, and long-term support
  including bathing, dressing, meals, medications, and mobility. The abstraction is
  what makes reviewers skim; the list is what makes them recognize their own parent.
- **Families do not fail for lack of information.** They fail between identifying
  appropriate care and establishing it. This is the spine's dichotomy and it holds.

### 2.2 Care navigation, and where preventive geriatric care sits

- **Care Navigation is the named function**, and it is a loop, not a lookup: assess
  needs and means, build a plan, execute the plan, track, update the profile,
  outcomes. Drawing it as a cycle rather than a funnel is what makes "execution and
  follow-up" visibly part of the product instead of a promise.
- **Preventive geriatric care is the frame above navigation**, not a second product.
  Navigation is how prevention is delivered to a household that has no geriatrician,
  no case manager, and no discharge planner assigned to it. Use the frame when the
  audience is clinical or payer-facing; do not turn it into a clinical claim.
- **The ecosystem has two halves and families must cross both**: how care gets paid
  for (public aid programs, insurance coverage) and where care comes from (healthcare
  services, long-term services and supports). Most tools serve one half. Naming the
  two halves explicitly is what makes the complexity legible in one glance.
- **Coverage, not features, is the competitive claim.** Every alternative solves one
  stretch of the path (assess needs, find aid and insurance, match providers,
  establish care, staff the care) and stops at a handoff. The advantage is that one
  system carries the family the whole way.

### 2.3 The caregiver-capacity bottleneck

- **Navigation alone fails, and the reason is arithmetic.** Aggregating family demand
  against a supply side that cannot staff it produces nothing but faster
  disappointment. 63.3 percent of home-care providers declined cases in 2023 they
  could not staff.
- **This is the strongest structural argument in the application** and it is what
  makes the workforce work non-optional rather than a second business. State it as a
  dependency: Aim 1 is useless if Aim 2 fails.

### 2.4 Workforce expansion, not recruitment from the existing pool

This is the sharpest conceptual gain of the whole exercise and the one most easily
lost in drafting.

- **Agencies, job boards, and gig platforms move workers between employers. The pool
  does not grow.** Every competing channel competes for the same workers. Olera's
  channel adds workers.
- **The innovation is not "we recruit students."** That is copyable and something
  close to it already exists. The innovation is **the infrastructure that turns a
  person who has never worked in eldercare into someone a licensed provider will
  hire, and that carries their verified record forward across employers.**
- **Students are the first population tested, not the definition of the strategy.**
  They were chosen because admission to health-professions programs requires
  documented patient-care hours, which makes recruitment cheap, and because Olera has
  operating experience and paid provider evidence with them. Other new-worker pools
  run the same pathway.
- **The verified record is the mechanism** (hours, employers, training, competencies,
  populations served, supervisor evaluations, reliability, credential status). It is
  what makes any pool viable and what compounds across employers. Internally this has
  been called the Senior Care Experience Passport; confirm the external name before
  it enters prose.
- **The provider employs, trains, insures, and supervises.** Olera recruits and
  vets. This is what keeps the work inside licensed care, and it is the answer to
  every safety and liability objection.
- **New cohorts arrive every season, so the pipeline refills.** Seasonality is a
  feature of the student channel, not a defect to hide.
- **Extensibility is claimed by design, not by a funded task** (ratified 2026-08-26).
  The pathway is population-agnostic; Aim 2 tests the campus dependency by running one
  market with a health-professions campus nearby and one without. A non-student worker
  pool is named as the first extension after the award and carries no endpoint here.
  The earlier plan for a bounded second-pool pilot in Year 3 is withdrawn.

### 2.5 Staffing is the beachhead, and it is the only product the award sells

- **One product is sold and priced during the award: Caregiver Staffing.** Providers
  pay because staffing is the binding constraint; unfilled shifts cap the clients
  they can take. Payment is per hire or monthly subscription.
- **Everything else is a measurement obligation, not a sales target.** A reviewer
  should be able to see that we know the difference.
- **Families never pay, and no provider pays to be listed.** No fee is charged for a
  referral, which is both the trust argument and the reason federally reimbursed
  providers (Medicaid, Medicare) can participate where pay-per-referral models must
  exclude them. This is a competitive advantage and a regulatory posture in one
  sentence, and it should be stated that way rather than as an anti-kickback
  footnote.
- **The beachhead is chosen because it is already revealed behavior.** Providers in
  the pilot paid. The pricing hypotheses come from that behavior, not from a draft.

### 2.6 Emerging payer customers, and the evidence they require

The distinction that organizes the entire commercial argument:

> **Beachhead customers get a product tested on them. Emerging customers get an
> evidence package built for them.**

- **The honest question is not "can we prove we reduce avoidable utilization" in
  three years.** It is "can we finish this award holding what a risk-bearing buyer
  requires before it will contract." The first framing overpromises and a reviewer
  will price the overpromise into the score.
- **The candidate customer classes** are Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid managed
  care organizations, health systems and ACOs, self-insured employers, long-term-care
  insurers, and public aging agencies. Each pays for the same underlying thing (a
  navigation episode carried to verified establishment) for a different reason.
- **The ladder a risk-bearing organization climbs, in order**, and what the award
  delivers:
  1. A measured case with verified care establishment. **Delivered (Aim 1).**
  2. A way to match a payer's members to the people we serve. **Delivered (Aim 3).**
  3. Our cases linked to a payer's own claims records. **Delivered (Aim 3).**
  4. Outcomes that matter to a payer: aid secured, needs met, time to care.
     **Delivered (Aim 1).**
  5. A cost model for what that care is worth. **Delivered (Aim 3).**
  6. Proof that it lowers hospital use and long-term care placement. **The award
     ends before this.** It is the first thing a post-award study measures.
- **Say where the award ends, on the page.** The credibility of items 1 to 5 comes
  from refusing item 6. This is the single most reviewer-legible honesty move in the
  deck and it should survive into the prose.
- **The stream's own milestone**: signed data-use agreements, demonstrated record
  linkage at a pre-specified match rate, a delivered cost model, and at least one
  letter of intent contingent on the evidence.

### 2.7 Operational evidence versus formal human-subjects research

- **Primary endpoints are operational or economic** and come from platform, billing,
  and employment records. Health-related outcomes appear only as secondary,
  observational measures on a cohort that all receives the service.
- **Three bounded IRB studies, one per aim**, and nothing more: family-caregiver
  usability and trust (Aim 1, n=25); provider and worker acceptability,
  appropriateness, and feasibility (Aim 2); a small qualitative adoption and
  price-fairness component (Aim 3).
- **Determinations are obtained, not assumed**, including for the streams we expect
  to be "not human subjects research," and they are listed in the Approach so a
  reviewer does not have to guess.
- **The project stays outside the NIH clinical-trial definition by design.** Nobody
  is prospectively assigned to receive or not receive navigation. Price arms are
  assigned to business accounts, and the outcome is a purchase, not a health
  outcome. Expert-panel review is internal product verification. Worker-facing
  research is firewalled from employment decisions in the protocol.
- **Care established is operational telemetry, never a clinical endpoint.** This is
  the standing Qiping boundary and the payer stream does not breach it.
- **No FDA pathway applies.** The product is navigational and administrative
  software, not a medical device, and no premarket authorization gates
  commercialization. What does govern: HIPAA-aligned privacy, referral and marketing
  practice, employment and supervision, and payer data linkage.

### 2.8 The Aim architecture

**Ratified 2026-08-26** (Logan and TJ). Supersedes the earlier product-axis architecture
in which Aims 1 and 2 split the family and provider products and ran in parallel. The axis
is now **maturity**, not product: engineer, pilot free, pilot paid. Both products move
through all three stages together.

| | What the aim asks | Gate |
|---|---|---|
| **Aim 1** (Year 1) | Can we engineer the technology and infrastructure both products require, well enough to put in front of families? | Month 12: expert-panel agreement 85 percent or higher, material errors under 10 percent, or no market activates |
| **Aim 2** (Years 1-2) | With families, providers, and caregivers concentrated in two markets at no charge, does care get established and does the staffing pathway complete? | Month 21: care established and the staffing path completing end to end, or wave one is held |
| **Aim 3** (Years 2-3) | In eight new paid markets, will providers pay, do the unit economics hold after real costs, and does the playbook repeat? | Month 30: interim price analysis and the stop rule |

- **The aims are questions with yes/no answers, not activity lists.** Activities cannot
  fail, so they cannot be hypotheses.
- **The sequence is the design, not a schedule.** Every parameter Aim 3 prices against is
  produced by Aim 2 at no commercial risk: baseline conversion, cost to serve, fill rate
  and time to hire, 90-day worker retention, and the playbook itself. Aim 3 is Aim 2 run
  larger, in markets we did not design in, with money changing hands.
- **Aim 1 ends with markets ready, not software alone** (Task 1.5, market selection and
  preparation), so the month 12 gate reads as a launch decision rather than a code freeze.
- **Aim 2 charges nothing**, so adoption is never confounded with price. Its free access
  is grandfathered for the life of the award; see 2.9.
- **Aim 3's prices start from what providers have already paid us**, about $275 a month
  and about $150 per placement, with a stated-preference instrument setting the range and
  revealed behavior deciding the price. We do not test arbitrary prices.
- **Aim 3 speaks standard business language**: revenue, unit economics, retention, churn,
  payback, margin. Targets are lifetime value to acquisition cost of 3:1 or better, payback
  under 12 months, and a positive per-market margin after the cost of serving that market's
  families, with an independent analyst rebuilding the economics from raw records.
- **A stop rule is stated on the page.** If, at the month 30 interim, worker retention and
  paid conversion both fail their thresholds, the provider-funded model is reported as
  disconfirmed and wave two is held.
- **The database learns from every completed case**, reported inside the aims rather than
  made its own aim: eligibility accuracy against the accumulating volume of executed cases.

### 2.9 Market design

- **Ten markets: two free in Aim 2, eight paid in Aim 3** (ratified 2026-08-26).
  Supersedes the twelve-market derivation previously recorded here.
- **Why the earlier twelve was wrong.** That derivation required roughly 300 *paying*
  accounts. A price experiment randomizes accounts *offered* a price, not accounts that
  convert, which overstated the requirement by about three times at a 35 percent
  conversion rate. The corrected derivation is below.
- **The two free markets (Aim 2)** differ on workforce source, one with a
  health-professions campus nearby and one without, because the riskiest assumption in
  the application is whether people new to care work can be recruited and kept. One
  market cannot separate a product that works from a market that happens to work.
- **The eight paid markets (Aim 3)** follow from the pricing experiment: four price
  points rather than two, because two show the direction of demand and four show the
  shape of the curve; two markets per arm, because price is assigned by market (neighbors
  compare quotes) so one market per arm means the arm *is* a market; and within each pair
  one campus-rich and one campus-poor market, so price is crossed with workforce source
  rather than confounded with it. Four arms times two types is eight.
- **Two waves of four.** Wave one at month 21 is the only wave with runway to observe
  12-month retention. Wave two at month 30 is run as written by staff who did not design
  it, which is the replication test.
- **Free pilot providers are grandfathered** for the life of the award. Conversion of the
  Aim 2 cohort is explicitly *not* an Aim 3 activity or endpoint. This keeps the paid test
  clean and removes any incentive for a pilot provider to shade their Aim 2 answers.
- **Socioeconomic status and urbanicity are stratification variables**, measured and
  reported, not design axes. That is what keeps eight from becoming sixteen.
- **A market is one county**, and the document says so at first use. Do not alternate
  between "market" and "county" as if the reader knows they are the same thing.
- **Budget**: roughly $30,000 to enter a market, so ten markets is about seven percent of
  the requested budget. The binding constraint is operating attention, not capital.
- **Stale elsewhere**: Commercialization Plan §9 still says 18 markets and the old
  ~300-account / ~$720K ARR planning estimate is not carried forward. Both need
  reconciling against this section.

### 2.10 What the award produces, and in what order

Four things, in a strict dependency order that should govern how the Approach closes
and how the Commercialization Plan opens:

1. **Technical validation** (Aim 1): the product does what it claims, verified
   against blinded expert review.
2. **Commercialization evidence** (Aims 2 and 3): providers adopt, pay, stay, and the
   unit economics are measured from real billing rather than modeled.
3. **Replication** (Aim 3): the model repeats in market types selected to include the
   ones where it should struggle. A model that only works where it was born is a
   consulting engagement, not a company.
4. **Investor readiness**: economics an outside analyst rebuilt from records, plus a
   payer evidence package that opens the next customer class.

The award-end statement is three phrases and a consequence: **a product that works, a
market model that repeats, evidence for investors and payers**, which lets Olera
operate on revenue it measured, raise on economics an investor can check, and begin
payer validation with the evidence in hand.

### 2.11 The CRP Progress Report framing

The strongest device found for II.4, and it doubles as the argument for why NIH should
fund the remaining work:

- **Nine years of NIH and company work retired five risks**, each named as a question
  a skeptic would have asked, each answered with what was actually built or measured,
  and each labeled with which funder bought the answer: **technical risk** (could the
  data be assembled), **user risk** (would families accept it), **demand risk** (would
  families come, and at what cost), **supply risk** (would providers show up), and
  **workforce risk** (could we recruit caregivers, and would anyone pay for them).
- **One risk is left, and it is the one that cannot be bought**: can one local market
  pay for itself, and can we repeat it. It has to be measured in real markets with
  real money changing hands. That is the CRP.
- **Label which funder retired which risk.** Two of the five (demand, workforce) were
  retired beyond the Phase II scope, with I-Corps and company funds. That is
  commercialization initiative on the record, and reviewers of a commercialization
  award notice it.

## 4. Working hypotheses

True as far as we can tell, load-bearing in the current design, and subject to
evidence or to what David, Marcia, TJ, and Qiping say about the deck.

- **Staffing revenue alone can carry a market to self-funding.** The illustrative
  build is roughly 25 providers at about $80K a year against the cost of serving that
  market's families. Every payer line in the model is beyond the award.
- **About 25 paying accounts is what a mature market holds.** The market count depends
  on this planning assumption more than on anything else in the derivation.
- **The pilot-derived prices are in the right neighborhood.** About $275 a month or
  about $150 a hire, from franchisees of four national brands and one assisted-living
  community who paid or agreed to pay.
- **Health-professions students convert into durable eldercare capacity.** Recruitment
  is demonstrated (more than 900 applicants, more than 20 placed). Retention at 90
  days and 12 months is not, and it is the honest attack on the workforce innovation.
- **A second worker pool runs the same pathway at comparable cost and retention.**
  Feasibility only, one Year 3 pilot, two markets.
- **Payers will contract on the evidence package.** The ladder in 2.6 is our model of
  what they require. It is reasoning from customer discovery, not from a signed
  contract, and the letters of intent are what would convert it.
- **Repeat use at 60 days of 50 percent or higher** and **accounts reaching a first
  verified paid shift of 70 percent or higher** are the staffing thresholds. Both were
  derived from the month-24 gate rather than measured.
- **The database is a durable moat.** A competitor who copies the features starts with
  an empty database, and the case-by-case outcome record is not in any public dataset.
  This is strongly argued and not yet tested by an actual competitor.
