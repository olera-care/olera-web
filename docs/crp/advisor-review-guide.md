# NIH Commercialization Readiness Pilot (CRP): Advisor Review Guide

**What the CRP is.** The CRP is an NIH SBIR/STTR follow-on mechanism for technologies that have progressed through Phase II or Phase IIB but still require late-stage development and commercialization work to reach the market. Awards run up to three years, with a current general ceiling of $4,191,495 in total support. The CRP is intended as the last planned NIH support: full commercialization must ultimately proceed with non-SBIR funds, so the application must show the company can attract third-party investment or sustain itself on revenue. The central question NIH asks reviewers to answer: can additional NIH funding move a promising, already-validated technology through its remaining "Valley of Death" to sustainable commercialization?

**What reviewers are asked to evaluate, document by document.**

- **Specific Aims (1 page).** The bridge between the Research Plan and the Commercialization Plan: each aim should remove an important remaining barrier to commercialization, carry measurable outcomes, and together leave the product at a point where commercialization can continue without SBIR funding.
- **Research Plan (12 pages).** How the proposed work leads to a marketable product or service, with quantitative milestones; the significance of the problem and the commercial opportunity, and the innovation relative to existing approaches; a rigorous approach with benchmarks, potential problems, and alternative strategies; and a progress report showing how prior Phase I/II/IIB work reduced commercialization risk.
- **Commercialization Plan (12 pages).** A realistic path that extends beyond the CRP to full commercialization: what commercialization gap remains and why NIH funding is needed now; the product, customers, market, and competition; IP position; the finance plan and revenue model; and five-year commercialization milestones that are realistic and market-based.

**The Fundraising Plan and the investor letter, in NIH's own words.**

> "The NIH considers the raising of independent third-party investor funds to be an important means to facilitate and accelerate the capital-intensive steps that are required to commercialize new products/technologies emerging from NIH-funded SBIR/STTR Phase II projects. Applicants are expected to provide a Fundraising Plan, which should be a detailed and specific plan for securing substantial, independent third-party investor funds if they are necessary to bring the product to commercialization. If they are not needed, the company should detail how they are able to bring the technology to market without the use of third-party investor funds."

> "In addition to standard letters of support documenting collaborations and access to expertise or unique research resources, applicants should include letters of support documenting commitments from third-party investors to support the Fundraising Plan section if applicable."

On the forms this evidence may take, the application guide states: "Plans for this financing stage may be demonstrated in one or more of the following ways: Letter of commitment of funding. Letter of intent or evidence of negotiations to provide funding, should the Phase II project be successful and the market need still exist. Letter of support for the project and/or some in-kind commitment (e.g., to test or evaluate the innovation). Specific steps you are going to take to secure Phase III funding." And reviewers are asked: "will third-party investors play an active role in facilitating the commercialization of the product/technology, and if so to what extent?" NIH thus accepts a range of evidence, from funding commitments to letters of support; what matters is that the letters credibly support the Fundraising Plan.

**How scoring works.** Reviewers score on NIH's 1-9 scale (1 = exceptional, 9 = poor). The core criteria (Significance, Investigators, Innovation, Approach, Environment) receive individual scores; CRP-specific considerations (the commercialization plan, fundraising plan, IP position, and regulatory path) inform the single Overall Impact score, which is not an average. The governing question: how likely is this project, if successfully completed, to exert a sustained, powerful influence on the relevant market offering?

**Calibrate your review around five questions.** 1. Is the remaining commercialization problem important, clear, and convincingly supported? 2. Do the Specific Aims directly resolve the barriers preventing commercialization? 3. Are the methods, milestones, and decision rules rigorous enough to produce credible evidence? 4. Is the commercialization plan realistic: market, customers, competition, revenue, financing, IP, five-year milestones? 5. If the CRP succeeds, is there a credible path from NIH support to a commercially sustainable product with meaningful impact?
