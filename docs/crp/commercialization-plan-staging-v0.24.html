COMMERCIALIZATION PLAN

1. Statement of Need (Table 1)

The product and its impact. Olera developed the CareNavigator through NIA Phase I–IIB support as a digital coordination layer that establishes eldercare by connecting families who need care, providers who deliver it, and the workforce that staffs it. Its potential impact comes from intervening in a major and growing public health problem in American eldercare: a vicious cycle in which unmet daily needs contribute to preventable hospitalizations, premature institutionalization, and rising public costs (Figure 1). The CareNavigator is particularly timely because this cycle is worsening as the population ages, Americans live longer with chronic illness, and care needs increasingly exceed available caregiving capacity.

Olera’s Valley of Death. The Olera CareNavigator is currently deployed across every U.S. county, with growing organic traction (Figure 2) and validated usability.6-10 These national demand signals demonstrate that families and providers use the system and seek the connections it is designed to facilitate. What they do not yet establish is whether Olera can concentrate that participation in local markets and convert the resulting value into revenue that sustains CareNavigator beyond government funding. The Commercialization Readiness Pilot (CRP) and its Specific Aims are therefore designed around three requirements for full commercialization:

  • Concentrate family participation within local markets.
  • Concentrate provider and workforce participation within those same markets.
  • Convert the resulting value into recurring revenue.

In other words, Olera’s Valley of Death is the risk that time or capital runs out before these three commercialization requirements are established (Figure 3).

Why government funding is critically needed, and what is delayed without it. The case for federal funding to commercialize CareNavigator rests on three arguments:

  • The commercial evidence needed to attract private investment does not yet exist; the CRP is designed to generate it. Investors who fund company growth look for evidence that a business model works: customers acquired at a predictable cost, revenue that recurs, and markets that generate more revenue than they cost to operate.18 Olera's conversations with age-tech investors Equitage and Ziegler, NIH and NSF I-Corps mentors, and NIA Entrepreneur-in-Residence John Reinhart have consistently reinforced this bar. The proposed CRP experiments are designed to produce exactly this evidence.
  • Federal support lets Olera generate this evidence while preserving the mission-critical constraints built into CareNavigator (Figure 4). Families use CareNavigator free, and no provider can pay to influence a recommendation. These constraints align the commercial model with NIA's mission and with how CareNavigator creates the greatest impact within the existing eldercare ecosystem (Section 10). CRP support allows monetization to be tested around these constraints rather than the constraints traded away for near-term revenue.
  • Without CRP support the work is not impossible, but it is significantly delayed. With limited internally generated revenue, and organic growth that builds national reach but only slowly produces the local concentration needed to validate value and revenue models,17 Olera could test roughly one market at a time instead of running the concentrated, parallel program proposed in the Research Strategy [model: CPA]. We estimate the same commercialization research would take five to ten years instead of the three proposed here, prolonging Olera's exposure to the Valley of Death and delaying the point at which CareNavigator can interrupt the vicious cycle described above at meaningful scale (Figure 1).

How CRP funding advances Olera to full commercialization. The proposed CRP is designed to rapidly concentrate family and provider participation in local markets and validate value and revenue models so that CareNavigator can proceed to full commercialization on non-government funds. Each Specific Aim closes one of the three commercialization requirements:

  • Aim 1 concentrates family participation: twelve pilot markets, acquisition channels tested against pre-registered cost and liquidity targets.
  • Aim 2 concentrates provider and workforce participation: providers activated through free working pilots; a credentialed workforce recruited in the same markets.
  • Aim 3 converts the resulting value into recurring revenue: pricing tested under real billing; conversion, retention, and unit economics independently validated.

Together, these outcomes from these aims create a path beyond government support: ongoing scaling of the CareNavigator funded by operating revenue and, when advantageous, accelerated by private growth capital.

2. Value of the SBIR Project, Expected Outcomes, and Impact

Value. The Phase I–IIB research (Impact Scores 20 and 25, respectively) produced three assets:

  • The CareNavigator, created and validated, now in demonstrated national use.
  • Knowledge of what interrupting the cycle of unmet need requires: care established, not just identified, with caregiving capacity behind it.
  • What providers will pay for, identified in their own words.

The value of this project is converting those research outputs into a sustainable commercial model, preserving the public benefit the product was designed to deliver, so the CareNavigator can reach its full impact potential. If successful, that model could help more older adults remain safely supported in their communities while reducing preventable healthcare use, premature institutionalization, and the burden of unmet eldercare needs on families and society.

The product to be commercialized. The first of these assets is the Olera CareNavigator, a coordination platform connecting the three sides of eldercare shown in Figure 5: families who need care, providers who deliver it, and the workforce that staffs it. Families use CareNavigator for free. AI agents guide them from screening through follow-up using Olera’s curated national database of providers and aid programs, while a persistent household profile allows them to return as needs change. Providers pay for the Provider Growth Suite: Staffing, Boost, and Conversion tools that help them hire staff, increase visibility, and convert prospective clients. The workforce component connects both sides by bringing new caregivers into provider employment. This design is grounded in Phase I–IIB research with caregivers, older adults, experts, and program administrators6-10 that mapped the eldercare ecosystem CareNavigator now serves.

The need and weaknesses in current approaches. The Phase I–IIB research addressed a large and growing need: 61 million Americans are age 65 or older, rising to 88.8 million by 2060,1 while families face persistent challenges navigating care, affording services, and finding available caregivers. The long-term care sector alone must fill 9.7 million direct-care job openings between 2024 and 2034.2 At the same time, an estimated $58 billion in aid for older adults goes unclaimed each year because families do not know programs exist, do not know they qualify, or cannot complete the application process.4 When these needs remain unmet, they can feed the vicious cycle shown in Figure 1: preventable complications, hospitalization, and premature institutionalization at substantial cost to families and the public.5

Breaking this cycle requires establishing support before unmet needs become crises. Our Phase I–IIB research identified five steps needed to do this: screening, matching, application support, intake, and follow-up (Figure 6), with re-engagement as needs change. We have found that existing approaches address parts of this sequence, but none address it end to end. Government resources such as Eldercare Locator, BenefitsCheckUp, and Area Agencies on Aging provide information and screening but generally do not execute applications or follow families through care establishment. Social workers and geriatric care managers can provide hands-on support, but access is limited and often episodic. Referral platforms connect families with participating providers but typically monetize through provider referral or placement fees. And even successful navigation can fail at the final step when a matched provider lacks the staff to deliver care.

Adding caregiving capacity. Better navigation alone cannot solve the caregiver shortage. More than 200 NIH and NSF customer-discovery interviews made this clear: providers reported needing staff as much as, and often more than, new clients. Olera has seen the same problem in practice, with providers declining referred families because they lack caregivers. This is an industry-wide constraint: 63.3% of home-care providers reported turning down cases because of staffing shortages in 2023.25

The 9.7 million projected openings will be difficult to fill from the existing labor pool alone. Caregiving wages compete with retail and warehouse jobs, provider margins limit wage growth, and annual turnover approaches 75%.3 Hiring an existing caregiver away from another provider shifts capacity rather than adding it. Our earlier work therefore explored ways to bring new workers into caregiving. Retirees, career changers, and students each offer potential, and a mature workforce strategy may draw from several groups. Students preparing for health careers provide a strong starting point because:

  • The work advances their careers: many health-professions programs value or expect meaningful patient-care experience.
  • The workforce renews continuously: colleges produce new cohorts throughout the year.
  • Their availability can complement provider needs: evenings, weekends, summers, and school breaks can overlap with difficult-to-fill shifts.

Students enter the workforce through licensed providers with vacant shifts, which interview, hire, train, supervise, and credential them before they provide care. Families therefore continue receiving services through the same licensed providers, while those providers gain a new source of caregiving capacity.

3. Company

Origins and objectives. Olera, Inc. was founded in 2020 out of Sling Health at Texas A&M University, a student-run medical-technology incubator whose local chapter Tokunbo (TJ) Falohun, the PI, launched during his biomedical-engineering doctorate to bring medicine, engineering, and business students together on healthcare problems. There he met Logan DuBose, MD, MBA, now a physician and the company’s co-investigator and chief operating officer, and the two began working on support for families caring for someone with dementia. That work surfaced a broader problem, that eldercare is hard to navigate and hard to afford, and Olera widened its scope to eldercare navigation: the problem the founders discovered is the product this plan commercializes.

The corporate objective has not changed since: make establishing eldercare navigable for every American family, free, and build the provider-side growth infrastructure that sustains that mission commercially. That objective fixes the operating rules this plan monetizes within: core navigation stays free to families, recommendations stay neutral, no payment gates a basic family-provider connection, and provider revenue comes only from incremental value-added services. Serviceable market. The buyers of Staffing, Boost, and Conversion are the care-delivery businesses counted in Table 6. Demonstrating a sustainable model at this scale would provide the evidence needed to support broader commercial expansion.

Potential future competitors: ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. General-purpose AI assistants will increasingly answer eldercare questions. Olera’s strategy is interoperability rather than direct competition: make CareNavigator’s provider database and application-execution capabilities accessible through emerging agent interfaces, allowing AI assistants to serve as entry points to CareNavigator rather than substitutes for it. As general information becomes easier to access, CareNavigator differentiates itself through grounded data and execution. Health systems and payers are also developing navigation tools, but these primarily serve their own populations and may represent distribution partners rather than direct platform competitors.

Significant advantages. Olera changes what is free and what providers pay for. Incumbent aggregators can charge providers $3,000–$6,000 per placement. Olera instead provides qualified family leads at no cost and offers paid tools around that demand: Boost increases provider visibility; Conversion helps providers convert leads from Olera, their own marketing, or other sources; and Staffing adds workforce capacity through the student pathway. If Olera can deliver lead quality comparable to incumbent aggregators in key private-pay segments, providers can receive qualified leads without sacrificing margin to placement fees. The CRP will test this model, and Aim 3 will determine whether and how much providers are willing to pay for these premium tools. Three potential advantages follow, supported by the durable assets in Figure 8:

  • New capability: free, neutral family demand paired with paid tools that help providers turn that demand into growth.
  • Lower cost: providers can receive qualified leads without the per-lead or per-placement fees that reduce margins.
  • More effective: families can arrive screened and matched to appropriate services, with applicable aid identified and applications underway, reducing the work between an initial inquiry and established care.

Market and customer acceptance hurdles. Four barriers could affect adoption, and the CRP is designed to address each. (1) Provider trust: providers may be wary of platforms after experience with per-lead and per-placement models. Olera allows providers to begin free and tests whether they will pay for added value after experiencing the product; Aim 3 measures this conversion directly. (2) Student caregiver safety: students are hired, trained, insured, and supervised by licensed providers under their existing employment standards. Franchisees of four national home-care brands have already participated in and paid for this model. (3) Family trust in AI navigation: usability and acceptance have been evaluated in peer-reviewed studies (Section 2), recommendations are grounded in Olera’s curated database, and human navigators are available for escalation. (4) Dependence on campus-by-campus operations: self-service portals are designed to make workforce recruitment and onboarding repeatable across campuses.

Strategic alliances and partnerships. Three types of existing relationships support commercialization. Distribution: franchisees of four national home-care brands already participate in the workforce pilot, creating a path to broader regional and national relationships if local results are strong. Workforce: university and student-organization relationships support caregiver recruitment. Research: Clemson University serves as a co-investigator and research collaborator, as described in Section 3. No licensing agreement is required to commercialize CareNavigator, and FDA approval is not applicable because the platform provides navigation and administrative support rather than functioning as a medical device.

Marketing and sales strategy. The Specific Aims define Olera’s market-entry strategy. As shown in Figure 9, the CRP will build each market by acquiring families, providers, and workforce participants; demonstrating value; and converting providers to paid products. Each market will refine a repeatable playbook that can then be applied to subsequent markets. 6. Intellectual Property Protection

Formal protections. Olera maintains trademarks on its marks, copyright in the platform and its content library, contractual protections in provider and partner agreements, and trade-secret protection of the database, curation pipelines, and workflow know-how [confirm registrations]. Software patents offer little durable protection in this category and are not the strategy; this plan does not overstate formal rights.

Temporal and operational barriers. The durable advantages of Figure 8 are protected by actions and structure, each constituting at least a temporal barrier to imitation:

  • Curated database: curation pipelines are trade secrets, access runs under contractual terms, and the follow-up loop accumulates outcomes data no competitor can reconstruct. The barrier is accumulation, not code.
  • Organic distribution: search and AI-search authority accrue only with years of content and use; a competitor starts from zero and must spend the same years.
  • Provider and workforce participation: claimed profiles, reviews, and verified student credentials create switching costs on both sides of the marketplace.
  • Neutral business model: the barrier is structural. A per-placement incumbent cannot offer free, neutral navigation without abandoning the revenue that funds it.

Regulatory position. Olera’s model is designed to reduce regulatory risk. Olera does not charge for referrals, avoiding the financial incentives that can create steering and kickback concerns for per-placement models in federally reimbursed markets [legal review]. Family data are managed under HIPAA-aligned safeguards, caregivers are employed and supervised by licensed providers, and VA claims are referred to accredited representatives as required by federal law.

7. Finance Plan

Capital required, and when. Olera is requesting approximately $4 million over three years, or $1.3–$1.4 million annually [final budget governs]. Funding is needed beginning at award and throughout the three-year project to support four activities: the commercialization research described in the Research Strategy; completion and refinement of the provider products; market entry across 12 pilot markets and approximately six paid-first markets, including paid acquisition needed to concentrate participation; and pricing, packaging, and unit-economics experiments with independent CPA validation. From CRP capital to revenue. The financing path is straightforward: CRP funding supports the proposed experiments; those experiments establish the commercial milestones described in Section 2; and those milestones provide the evidence needed to generate recurring revenue with a measured cost structure. After the award, spending shifts from intensive research and validation toward market expansion, sales and marketing, provider success, platform operations, and continued product development. Section 10 defines the post-award operating baseline and the revenue required to support it. Under the Section 10 projections (Figure 10), revenue reaches that baseline as the award ends; the pace of later market expansion adjusts to available margin. The long-term financing model is operating revenue. Private investment, if secured, accelerates growth but is not required to sustain the business.

Post-award financing and supporting evidence. Olera plans to pursue a growth round of at least $4 million near the end of the award to accelerate market expansion. 9. Production and Marketing Plan

How production occurs. Olera produces CareNavigator entirely in-house, with the W-2 engineering and product personnel who have built and operated the platform through every prior SBIR phase. Production spans software development and deployment; AI-agent development grounded in the curated database; curation workflows adding roughly 100,000 profiles per year; product instrumentation and quality assurance; and security and privacy controls over family data. Because the product is software rather than physical manufacturing, production scales economically: marginal cost per additional family or provider is low, hosting and inference are measured under Aim 3, and no manufacturing, inventory, or distributor dependencies exist. Call-center navigators, four years on the project, deliver the human escalation layer, and the provider and student portals are self-service by design.

Steps to market and sell. Marketing and sales are internal capabilities, run by a marketing and outreach team with more than three years on the project; CRP funding systematizes and scales what this team already does, through the acquisition system of Figure 9. Family acquisition runs the Figure 9 channels under Aim 1’s pre-registered decision rules. Provider sales work an asset competitors lack: the product database is the prospect list, so selling is claim-your-profile outreach, free pilot activation, then measured conversion to paid subscription, self-serve online nationally and direct in active markets (Aim 2); no licensing or distribution intermediaries are required. Workforce recruitment runs through the campus channels of Figure 9. After local proof, an enterprise motion sells regional and national agreements to multi-location and franchise networks (Section 8).

10. Revenue Stream

How the revenue model works. Olera generates revenue by direct sales and service: provider subscriptions and placement fees, sold directly by Olera. The model has four parts:

  • Families use CareNavigator free, permanently.
  • Providers receive qualified family leads free.
  • Providers pay for three products when they create additional value: Staffing supplies caregivers through the healthcare-student pathway, priced per successful placement or as an unlimited-hiring subscription; Boost raises a provider’s visibility beyond the free base leads; Conversion turns leads from any source into admitted clients through follow-up and intake automation.
  • Aim 3 determines final pricing and packaging.

What still must be proven. Providers demonstrably value these problems: they pay incumbent platforms thousands of dollars per new client, and franchisees of four national home-care brands paid for the workforce pilot. Those are willingness-to-pay signals, not a validated price list. Aim 3 establishes the commercial model directly: which products providers pay for, at what price and in what packages, whether they remain paying customers, and whether the resulting revenue exceeds the cost of delivering and selling the products. The results of those experiments set the model used in the projections below.

How the projection is built. One market is one county, and Table 9 gives its economics: entering costs ≈$30,000 (provider outreach and onboarding, launch marketing, and campus-partner setup), serving costs ≈$1,000 a month (account management and provider support), and a market reaches its mature revenue of ≈$2,500 a month over about 18 months. The CRP opens all 18 markets early: 5 in Year 1 and the rest by the end of Year 2, with the final six opening as soon as Aim 3 pricing begins, so that every market is at or near mature revenue when the award ends. Paid conversion begins mid-Year 2, and churn is assumed offset by continuing conversion of the activated pool, an Aim 3 hypothesis.

Reading the projection. The award funds three years of operating costs while the 18 markets are built and the model is validated. Commercial revenue grows through Years 2 and 3 as markets mature, and by the end of the award it runs at ≈$0.87 million a year, enough to cover the entire post-award cost of the company and its 18 markets. The CRP is sequenced to produce exactly this handoff: award funding carries Olera through validation, commercial revenue grows during the award, and the business pays for itself from the day the award ends, with no financing gap. If Aim 3’s measured economics come in weaker than assumed, the sustainability gate measures the true threshold and expansion waits until margin allows it.

Growth without outside capital. Olera does not need investment to keep operating or growing. A new market costs ≈$40,000 in its first year and returns ≈$18,000 a year once mature, so each year’s margin pays for the next year’s markets. That pace compounds, from two new markets in 2031 to more than ten a year by 2037, reaching ≈70 markets, ≈1,500 paying providers, and ≈$2.8 million in revenue (Figure 10, solid line).

What growth capital buys. The Section 8 raise of at least $4 million funds the same validated playbook, faster. Deployed over two years, it opens ≈60 additional markets and the sales and support capacity to run them: by 2032, ≈80 markets, ≈1,300 paying providers, and ≈$2.7 million in revenue, roughly twice the self-funded path (Figure 10, dashed line). By 2037 the same pace reaches ≈180 markets and ≈$5.7 million in revenue, still under 4% of the 72,000+ providers Olera has already indexed. These figures state what the capital buys in markets, providers, and revenue, using the per-market economics the CRP will have validated; they are not a promised investor return, which would require modeling valuation and exit terms.

How Olera’s team changes as revenue grows. During the CRP, Olera’s research-intensive team will run the proposed experiments and complete the provider products. As the CRP ends, the team will shift from research and validation toward market operations, sales, provider success, and platform support. As revenue and the number of markets grow, Olera will add market and customer-success capacity as needed, while engineering grows more slowly because the platform and self-service tools are designed to support increasing volume efficiently. After the CRP, operating revenue will support continued commercialization, while private investment, if secured, will accelerate expansion rather than sustain core operations (Sections 7 and 8). No SBIR funds are used for post-CRP commercialization.
