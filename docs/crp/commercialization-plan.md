# Commercialization Plan (working snapshot, 2026-08-17)

## 1. STATEMENT OF NEED

The product and its impact. Olera developed the CareNavigator through NIA Phase I–IIB as a digital eldercare coordination layer that connects families who need care, providers who deliver it, and the workforce that staffs it. Its impact potential comes from intervening in a major public health problem in America: a vicious cycle in which unmet daily needs contribute to preventable geriatric hospitalizations, premature institutionalization, and rising public costs (Figure 1). The CareNavigator is timely because this cycle is worsening as people live longer with more chronic illness, and care needs increasingly exceed available caregiving capacity. Figure 1: The vicious cycle CareNavigator interrupts.5

Olera's Valley of Death (Figure 3). CareNavigator is currently deployed across the U.S. with growing organic traction (15K+ users per month) and validated usability.6-10 Key demand signals demonstrate that families and providers seek the connections it is designed to facilitate. Olera's Valley of Death is the risk that capital runs out before this nationwide demand is converted into revenue to sustain the business. Therefore, our CRP specific aims are centered around this target:

- Concentrate family participation in local markets (Aim 1)
- Concentrate provider and workforce participation within those same markets (Aim 2)
- Convert the demand into recurring revenue (Aim 3)

Why government funding is needed to prevent delays. Government funding is needed for three reasons:

1. The evidence private investors need do not yet exist. Investors look for evidence that an established business model is profitable. Olera's conversations with age-tech investors Equitage and Ziegler, NIH and NSF I-Corps mentors, and NIA Entrepreneur-in-Residence John Reinhart have consistently reinforced this bar. However, Olera needs capital and time to establish the business model. CRP mechanism is designed to provide that time and capital to develop the evidence investors need.

2. Olera has built mission-critical constraints into CareNavigator (Figure 4). Families use CareNavigator free. This constraint, among three others shown in Figure 4, aligns with how CareNavigator creates the greatest impact within the existing eldercare ecosystem. CRP support allows monetization around these constraints rather than the constraint pressure from the need for near-term revenue or returns.

3. Without CRP support, the work is significantly delayed. Our organic growth builds diffuse national traction to the platform but only slowly produces the local concentration needed to validate value and revenue models.17 Olera could test roughly one market at a time instead of running the accelerated program proposed in the Research Strategy, where we will activate multiple markets in parallel. We estimate the same commercialization research would take five to ten years longer instead of the three proposed in this CRP timeline. This would prolong Olera's exposure to the Valley of Death and delay the point at which CareNavigator can interrupt the vicious cycle (Figure 1). Figure 4: Mission-critical constraints; monetization is tested around them. Figure 3: Olera's Valley of Death and three specific aims to cross it.

How CRP funding advances Olera to full commercialization. Our CRP proposal funds three specific aims designed to rapidly concentrate family and provider users in local markets and validate value and revenue models towards full commercialization. Together, these aims create a path to full commercialization and sustainability beyond government support:

- Aim 1 concentrates families seeking care in local markets: twelve pilot markets (i.e., counties) where we will test acquisition channels and validate the value added by helping families establish care.
- Aim 2 concentrates eldercare providers and new caregiving capacity: In the same markets, we will test marketing channels to acquire new provider users and observe the value created by connecting them with (1) qualified families from Aim 1 and (2) new staff recruited from local labor pools.
- Aim 3 converts the resulting value into revenue: Once value add for providers is clear, qualified client and staff connections will be packaged, paywalled, and priced, and we will observe conversion, retention, and unit economics. Once offerings are profitable, we will expand to new markets.

## 2. VALUE OF THE CRP PROJECT, EXPECTED OUTCOMES, AND IMPACT

Value of the CRP Project. The Phase I–IIB research (Impact Scores 20 and 25, respectively) produced three assets:

1. A first-generation CareNavigator validated for usability and in use by families and providers nationwide
2. Knowledge of the cycle of unmet need in geriatric populations and what is needed to stop it
3. What providers in the eldercare ecosystem need and will pay for: qualified new clients and staff leads

This CRP converts these earlier research outputs into commercial value while preserving the public benefit the CareNavigator was designed to deliver. The CareNavigator, how it meets the needs of the eldercare ecosystem, and our plan to translate its commercial value into a sustainable business are discussed next.

The product to be commercialized. The CareNavigator is a coordination platform connecting the three sides of eldercare shown in Figure 5: families who need care, providers who deliver it, and the workforce that staffs it. Families use CareNavigator for free. AI agents guide them from needs and eligibility screening through follow-up to confirm care establishment. It pulls from a curated national database of providers and aid programs developed over Olera's Phase I-IIB research. Providers may join the network for free but are offered the option to pay for premium Provider Growth Suite tools, including tools to help improve their staffing, visibility to acquire more family leads, and conversion tools that help them convert prospective leads into paying clients. This design is grounded in our SBIR research with caregivers, older adults, experts, and program administrators6-10 that mapped the eldercare ecosystem and specified how the CareNavigator could best serve to reduce the pain points of families seeking eldercare and care providers. Figure 5: The CareNavigator platform and what its database connects families to.

The need for CareNavigator and weaknesses in current approaches. 61 million Americans are age 65 or older, rising to 88.8 million by 2060,1 and it is well established that families face persistent challenges navigating eldercare, affording services, and finding available caregiving arrangements as eldercare needs increase over the course of aging. The long-term care sector must fill 9.7 million caregiving job openings between 2024 and 2034,2 indicating a large shortage of qualified help for the elderly's necessities, including bathing, toileting, feeding, medication administration, among other activities of daily living (ADLs). When these needs are unmet, they can feed the vicious cycle shown in Figures 1 and 6: preventable complications, hospitalization, and premature institutionalization at substantial cost to families and the public.5 Figure 6: Without navigation the cascade runs; CareNavigator establishes support before needs go unmet and re-engages as needs evolve.

Breaking this cycle requires establishing support before unmet needs become crises. Our Phase I–IIB research identified five steps needed to do this: screening, matching, application support, intake, and follow-up (Figure 6), with re-engagement as needs change. We have found that existing approaches address parts of this sequence, but none address it end to end. For example, government resources such as Eldercare Locator, BenefitsCheckUp, and Area Agencies on Aging provide information and screening but generally do not execute applications or follow families through care establishment. Social workers and geriatric care managers can provide hands-on support, but access is limited and often episodic. Online referral platforms like APlaceForMom connect families with participating providers but typically monetize through provider referral or placement fees that biases recommendations and limit options to only in-network private-pay eldercare services. Further, even successful navigation and connection to a provider of care can fail when a matched provider lacks the staff to deliver the care.

Adding caregiving capacity. More than 200 customer-discovery interviews in the NIH and NSF Innovation-Corps program made the caregiver shortage clear: providers reported needing staff as much as, and often more than, new clients. Olera has seen the same problem in practice, with providers declining referred families because they lack caregivers to staff shifts. This is an industry-wide constraint: 63.3% of home-care providers reported turning down cases because of staffing shortages in 2023.25 The 9.7 million projected openings will be difficult to fill from the existing labor pool alone. Caregiving wages compete with retail, service, and warehouse jobs, provider margins limit wage growth, and tough working conditions cause annual turnover to approach 75%.3. Our research team therefore explored ways to bring new workers into caregiving. Retirees, career changers, and college students each offer potential, and a mature workforce strategy may draw from several groups to increase caregiving capacity. However, college students preparing for health careers are a particularly strong beachhead because:

- Caregiving advances their careers, and many health-profession programs like medical, nursing, physician assistant, or physical therapy value or expect meaningful patient-care experience in college.
- The workforce renews continuously as colleges produce new cohorts throughout the year.
- Their availability can complement provider needs: evenings, weekends, summers, and school breaks can overlap with difficult-to-fill shifts.
- Colleges are geographically distributed nationwide, even in many rural areas where shortages are [sentence truncated in source]

Based on this understanding, we piloted a program for health care college students that placed them with local providers with caregiving job openings and found positive results: 900 student applications, 100 accepted, and 25 placed, with both students and providers reporting success and returning to the program the next semester. Students enter the workforce through these licensed providers with vacant shifts, which interview, hire, train, supervise, and credential them before they provide care. Families therefore continue receiving services through the same licensed providers, while those providers gain a new source of caregiving capacity, all in a safe process.

## 3. COMPANY

Origins and objectives. Olera, Inc. was founded in 2020 out of Sling Health at Texas A&M University, a student-run medical-technology incubator whose local chapter Tokunbo (TJ) Falohun, the PI, launched during his biomedical-engineering doctorate to bring medicine, engineering, and business students together on healthcare problems. There he met Logan DuBose, MD, MBA, now a primary care physician and the company's co-investigator and chief research officer, and the two began working on support for families caring for someone with dementia. That work surfaced a broader problem: that eldercare is hard to navigate and hard to afford, and Olera widened its scope to eldercare navigation.

The corporate objective has not changed since: make establishing eldercare navigable for every American family, free, and build the provider-side growth infrastructure that sustains that mission commercially. That objective fixes the operating rules this commercialization plan monetizes within: core navigation stays free to families, recommendations stay neutral, no payment gates a basic family-provider connection, and provider revenue comes only from value-added services. ## 6. INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY PROTECTION

Formal protections. Olera maintains trademarks on its marks, copyright in the platform and its content library, contractual protections in provider and partner agreements, and trade-secret protection of the database, curation pipelines, and workflow know-how. Software patents offer little durable protection in this category and are not the strategy.

Regulatory position. Olera's model is designed to reduce regulatory risk. Olera does not charge for referrals, avoiding the financial incentives that can create steering and kickback concerns for per-placement models in federally reimbursed markets. Family data are managed under HIPAA-aligned safeguards. Caregivers are employed and supervised by licensed providers. Olera does not employ caregivers directly and is not a licensed home care agency. No licensing agreements are required to commercialize CareNavigator, and FDA approval is not applicable because the platform provides navigation and administrative support rather than functioning as a medical device.

## 8. PRODUCT AND MARKETING PLAN

Production spans software development and deployment; AI-agent development grounded in the curated database; curation workflows; product instrumentation and quality assurance; and security and privacy controls over family data. Call-center navigators, four years on the project, deliver customer support, and the provider and caregiver portals are self-service by design.

Steps to market and sell. Marketing and sales are internal capabilities, run by a marketing and outreach team with more than three years on the project; CRP funding systematizes and scales what this team already does, through the acquisition system of Figure 9. Workforce recruitment runs through the campus channels of (Section 5; Figure 9). After local proof, a strategic partnership and enterprise-focused effort sells regional and national agreements to expand to multi-location and franchise networks quickly and efficiently.

## 9. REVENUE STREAMS

How the revenue model works. Olera plans to generate revenue by direct sales to providers of eldercare. Aim 3 determines final pricing and packaging and the viability of these premium products. The revenue model we will test has three parts:

- Families use CareNavigator for free to connect with care providers and the aid service to pay for them, including home care agencies, senior living communities or other players in the eldercare ecosystem
- Providers receive qualified family leads free of charge to avoid steering or gate-keeping
- Providers pay for three premium products for additional value: Staffing supplies caregivers through the healthcare-student pathway, priced per successful placement or as an unlimited-hiring subscription; Boost raises a provider's visibility beyond the free base leads; Conversion turns leads from any source into admitted clients through follow-up and intake automation.

A new market costs ≈$40,000 in its first year and returns ≈$18,000 a year once mature, so each year's margin pays for the next year's markets. That pace compounds, from two new markets in 2031 to more than ten a year by 2037, reaching ≈70 markets, ≈1,500 paying providers, and ≈$2.8 million in revenue (Figure 10, solid line).

What growth capital buys. The Section 8 raise of at least $4 million funds the same validated playbook, faster. Deployed over two years, it opens ≈60 additional markets and the sales and support capacity to run them: by 2032, ≈80 markets, ≈1,300 paying providers, and ≈$2.7 million in revenue, roughly twice the self-funded path (Figure 10, dashed line). By 2037 the same pace reaches ≈180 markets and ≈$5.7 million in revenue, still under 4% of the 72,000+ providers Olera has already indexed. These figures state what the capital buys in markets, providers, and revenue, using the per-market economics the CRP will have validated; they are not a promised investor return, which would require modeling valuation and exit terms.

How Olera's team changes as revenue grows. During the CRP, Olera's research-intensive team will run the proposed experiments and complete the provider products. As the CRP ends, the team will shift from research and validation toward market operations, sales, provider success, and platform support. As revenue and the number of markets grow, Olera will add market and customer-success capacity as needed, while engineering grows more slowly because the platform and self-service tools are designed to support increasing volume efficiently. After the CRP, operating revenue will support continued commercialization, while private investment, if secured, will accelerate expansion rather than sustain core operations (Sections 7 and 8). No SBIR funds are used for post-CRP commercialization.
