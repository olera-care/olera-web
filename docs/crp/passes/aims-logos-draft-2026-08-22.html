SPECIFIC AIMS

Background. Older adults increasingly need help with the ordinary tasks of daily living, while the workforce, the family capacity, and the means to pay for that help are all shrinking. When support is not established, the consequences compound: preventable emergency care, caregiver breakdown, and premature institutionalization. Families on this path do not fail for lack of information. They fail between identifying appropriate care and establishing it: finding what aid or insurance can pay (an estimated $58 billion in assistance goes unclaimed each year1), and reaching a provider with capacity to start. Clinicians, information tools, and referral services each support part of this pathway; none is accountable for whether care is established. Nor can families finish alone: care is established only when a provider can say yes, and 63.3 percent of home-care providers declined cases in 2023 for lack of staff.2

The model and the open question. Across NIA SBIR Phases I-IIB we built CareNavigator, AI-agent technology performing the identification half of this pathway: screening a household's needs and matching it to aid, services, and providers from an expert-curated database. Around it runs a two-sided marketplace with one commercial rule: families use it free, and no one pays for a referral. Because no provider pays to be seen, every provider is listed, families are not steered, and federally reimbursed providers, excluded by pay-per-referral models, participate. The marketplace concentrates families and providers; revenue comes from two Provider Tools sold into that demand: Managed Ads (client acquisition) and Staff Recruitment (caregivers from health-professions students, employed by licensed providers). SBIR work established feasibility and demand: 15,500+ monthly organic visitors, 725+ onboarded providers, and a staffing pilot where providers paid for caregivers we recruited. What no one knows is whether the model closes: can the system reliably carry families from recognized need to established aid and care, and does that activity create enough value that the tools sell at prices that sustain the platform? No incumbent will run this experiment (a yes overturns the referral-fee model), and no pre-revenue company can run it unaided: that gap is the CRP's purpose.

CRP Objective. We will bring Olera to full commercial operation in twelve county markets, in the sequence the hypothesis requires: recruit families at measured cost; carry them to established aid and care; let that activity, and new student caregivers, make each marketplace valuable to its providers; then measure whether what providers pay covers the cost to acquire and serve that market: the self-funding condition. Every aim carries pre-registered bars and pre-committed alternatives: where the condition holds, markets open on reinvested earnings; where it fails, we learn which link broke.

Specific Aim 1 (Years 1-2). Determine whether the system can establish care, not only identify it, and at what cost. Families identify aid and care on the platform today but are lost executing applications and again after filing (Preliminary Work). We will (1) build the execution and follow-up loops that carry each case to a decision and confirm from platform records that care was established, verified against blinded expert review, (2) validate the integrated system with family caregivers under IRB approval, and (3) recruit families in all twelve markets, measuring acquisition cost and cost to serve. Result: the establishment rate and family economics the later links depend on, and a repeatable acquisition playbook.

Specific Aim 2 (Years 1-2). Determine whether establishment activity creates provider value, and what providers pay. We will (1) instrument and validate the two Provider Tools, (2) measure whether consented family-provider connections complete reliably (response rate and time, care established) and whether student recruiting adds the capacity to say yes, and (3) measure willingness to pay for both tools at increasing levels of marketplace activity. Result: whether the marketplace produces value providers recognize, and how willingness to pay moves with activity.

Specific Aim 3 (Years 2-3). Determine whether provider revenue can sustain a market under real billing, independently verified. Tools and markets advance into Aim 3 only where willingness to pay exceeds cost to serve. We will (1) set price and packaging through pre-registered experiments under real billing, (2) measure realized economics: conversion, revenue per account, cost to serve, retention, churn, and (3) validate each market through an independent financial rebuild. Result: a yes or no on the self-funding condition, market by market, with recurring revenue and an investor-ready evidence package where it holds.

Three years from now. Success means knowing, with independently verified numbers, what no one knows today: whether AI navigation can complete care establishment, at what rate; what a family costs to acquire and serve; whether marketplace activity drives willingness to pay, at what prices; whether students are a genuine new caregiving workforce; and whether any market reaches self-funding. The award leaves a verified playbook and recurring revenue; Olera expands beyond NIH support on earnings, private capital, or both; CareNavigator stays free for the families it serves.
