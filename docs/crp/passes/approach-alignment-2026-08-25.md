## 0. Innovation 2, broadened: agreed, with one addition

David is right and the reason he is right is worth stating precisely. "We recruit
students" is copyable, and something close to it already exists. "We recruit from
several pools" is also copyable, because it is just multi-channel recruiting. The
defensible innovation is the layer underneath both: **the infrastructure that turns
someone who has never worked in eldercare into a person a licensed provider will
hire, and that carries their verified record forward.** That is what makes any pool
viable, and it is what compounds.

Recommended architecture:

- **Innovation 2 is workforce expansion infrastructure.** Bringing new people into
  senior care and making them qualified, trusted, and employable.
- **The Senior Care Experience Passport is the mechanism**, not a side idea. Verified
  patient-care hours, employers worked with, training completed, competencies,
  populations served including dementia, supervisor evaluations, reliability, and
  background and credential status, accumulating across employers.
- **Students are the beachhead population**, chosen for testing because Olera has
  operating experience, a repeatable university channel, paid provider evidence, and a
  documented career incentive that makes recruitment cheap.
- **Figures 1 to 14 need no change.** They already say New Caregiver Workforce, and
  Figure 13's flow (new people, licensed provider employs, workforce grows) is
  population-agnostic as drawn. Only Figure 13's subtitle names students, and that is
  accurate as the tested population.

**This is not scope creep.** The Research Strategy already commits to the Passport in
substance: Aim 2 says "the student logs shift hours, the provider confirms them, and
Olera compiles a verified experience record." The re-base names it, generalizes it
beyond students, and makes it a measured deliverable.

**One addition to make extensibility evidence rather than assertion.** Run a bounded
**second-pool feasibility pilot in Year 3** in two markets, most usefully in
campus-poor markets where students are unavailable. Career changers or recent retirees
are the likeliest candidates. Small n, feasibility endpoints only: can the pathway
recruit, verify, and place a non-student cohort at comparable cost and retention. This
converts "extensible in principle" into "extensible, demonstrated once," and it costs
one task, not one aim.

**Consequence for Innovation 3.** The Passport feeds the same database as the family
side, so Innovation 3 becomes the record of both what programs decide and who can
deliver care. That strengthens Figure 14 rather than complicating it.

## 1. Final Aim architecture

**Aim 1. Establish care reliably, and prove it was established.** *(Years 1 to 2)*
Central question: **can an agentic system carry a household from screened need to
established aid or care, at a quality a licensed professional would sign, at a cost
that falls?**
Plain purpose: finish the half of the pathway Phase IIB did not build, and prove it
works well enough to sell.

**Aim 2. Bring new people into care work, and make providers want them.** *(Years 1 to 3)*
Central question: **can we add caregivers who were not already in the field, make them
employable through verified experience, and will providers pay for that?**
Plain purpose: relieve the bottleneck that would otherwise make Aim 1 useless, and
establish the beachhead product.

**Aim 3. Show the economics hold, replicate, and are bankable.** *(Years 2 to 3)*
Central question: **what will providers actually pay, do the market economics work
after real costs, and do we finish holding what a risk-bearing buyer requires?**
Plain purpose: convert demonstrated value into a validated business model plus the
evidence package the next customer class needs.

**The clinical-trial line.** Trials are allowed but optional; we are choosing to stay
outside the definition. That is achieved by never prospectively assigning families to
receive or not receive navigation. Health-related outcomes appear only as secondary
observational measures on a cohort that all receives the service, compared against
matched external data. Price arms are assigned to business accounts, not to patients,
and the outcome is purchase, not health.

## 4. Market count, derived twice

The number is not chosen. Two independent requirements set it, and they converge.

**Route one: the pricing experiment sets a floor on accounts, which sets a floor on
markets.** The primary pricing evidence is randomized offer prices under real billing,
assigned at the account level. Detecting a commercially meaningful difference in paid
conversion, on the order of 15 percentage points, requires roughly 150 accounts per
arm. Two arms per structure gives a requirement of about 300 paying accounts. At the
planning assumption of about 25 paying accounts in a mature market, that is **about
12 markets**. Note what this does and does not fix: the binding quantity is accounts,
not markets. The pre-registered rule should size on accounts, so if markets run richer
than 25 accounts, fewer markets suffice and the saved entry cost goes to depth.

**Route two: heterogeneity and payer clustering.** Three structural axes plausibly
change the economics, and geography is not one of them:

1. **State LTSS and waiver generosity** (high, low). Drives aid dollars secured,
   establishment rate, and how interesting the market is to a Medicaid buyer.
2. **Provider density and urbanicity** (metro, small metro or rural). Drives how many
   providers exist to sell to and how hard staffing is.
3. **Workforce-source availability** (campus-rich, campus-poor). Campus-poor markets
   are where the second-pool pilot runs, so this axis tests the extensibility claim
   rather than avoiding it.

Two levels on three axes is eight cells. **Eight replication markets, one per cell**,
is the minimum that tests the playbook where it should work and where it should
struggle. Add **four anchor markets** carrying the IRB studies, the deep cost
instrumentation, and the mechanism work, selected where existing traffic and provider
base are strongest. **Four plus eight is twelve.**

**The payer dimension changes composition, not count.** Risk-bearing organizations
contract regionally, so evidence for them requires episodes concentrated inside one
plan's footprint, not spread thin. Therefore markets are selected in **clusters of
three inside two payer footprints**, and those six markets do double duty filling
heterogeneity cells. Spreading twelve markets across twelve unrelated regions would
satisfy route two and destroy the payer evidence, which is why composition has to be
stated alongside the number.

**Conclusion.** Twelve, derived twice and for different reasons: the account floor the
pricing experiment needs, and the heterogeneity grid plus anchors. The composition is
4 anchors + 8 replication markets, of which 6 sit as two clusters of 3 inside payer
footprints. If a budget cut forces fewer, the defensible reduction is to 10 by
dropping two replication cells and accepting a descriptive rather than inferential
price contrast; the anchors and the payer clusters are the last things to cut.

## 5. Pricing methodology, final

**Starting hypotheses from behavior, not from a draft.** The Texas A&M pilot produced
paid placements, which is revealed behavior. Two structures follow: about $275 per
month for unlimited hiring, or about $150 per successful hire. Both are hypotheses
entering the design, neither is an answer leaving it.

| Stage | When | Method | What it decides | Evidence class |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Year 1 Q4 | Structured willingness-to-pay interviews with pilot users, plus Van Westendorp for the acceptable band and the too-cheap floor | The plausible range only | Stated, bias documented |
| 2 | Year 2 Q1 | Gabor-Granger over a shortlist, and a discrete-choice experiment crossing structure (subscription versus per-hire) with service level | The **structure**, which a field experiment cannot afford to test | Stated, but choice-based |
| 3 | Year 2 Q2 to Year 3 Q1 | **Randomized offer prices under real billing**, account level, stratified by market and provider size, two price points per structure, pre-registered | The **price** | Revealed, primary |
| 4 | Year 3 Q2 | Renewal and price-change test on existing accounts | Elasticity and retention at price | Revealed, secondary |

Primary outcome is paid conversion within 60 days of offer. Secondary outcomes are
retention at 6 and 12 months and revenue per account, because a price that converts
and then churns is worse than one that does neither. Contamination is the standard
objection to account-level assignment; individualized quotes are normal in business
software, and the pre-registered mitigations are a contamination monitor, non-adjacent
market pairing, and market-level assignment as a sensitivity analysis.

Stated-preference methods are documented to overstate willingness to pay, which is
exactly why stages 1 and 2 bound and structure the question while stage 3 answers it.

## 6. Timetable, derived backward

Each row states a requirement for May 31, 2030 and the scheduling it forces.

| Must be true at award end | Forces |
|---|---|
| Retention measured, not modeled, with restricted mean survival at 12 months | Paid conversion opens no later than **Year 2 Q2 (autumn 2028)** |

| A price the company operates on, plus elasticity | Field experiment **Y2 Q2 to Y3 Q1**, renewal test **Y3 Q2** |
| Payer package with demonstrated linkage and a first read | Episode cohort accrues from **Y2 Q1**; data-use agreements signed by **Y2 Q4** |
| Worker retention at 12 months, and extensibility demonstrated | First placements by **Y1 Q4**; second-pool pilot opens **Y3 Q1** |
| Independently rebuilt model and investor package | Rebuild **Y3 Q3**, package **Y3 Q4** |

**Year 1, build and verify.** Execution and follow-up loops built. Expert-panel
verification. IRB usability and trust study. Four anchor markets open, two in Q2 and
two in Q4. Staffing pipeline verified and live in anchors; first placements by Q4.
Passport record structure deployed. Pricing stage 1 in Q4. *Gate at month 12.*

**Year 2, replicate and price.** Eight replication markets in two waves, Q1 and Q3.
Pricing stage 2 in Q1; paid conversion with randomized offer prices from Q2. Episode
cohort accrues from Q1; data-use agreements signed by Q4. Staffing at volume across
clusters. *Gate at month 24.*

**Year 3, prove and hand off.** Retention and churn mature. Second-pool pilot in two
campus-poor markets from Q1. Renewal and price-change test Q2. Independent rebuild and
actuarial model Q3. First matched-comparison read Q3. Investor package Q4. *Final
gates.*
