North Star

What Olera exists to do

The capacity to care for America's aging population already exists. It is stranded: in aid no one claims, in workers who never enter the field, and in providers turning away families they could serve. Olera releases it, market by market, and every completed case teaches the system how to release it faster.

Why this over the alternative. David's version describes the mechanism accurately and its closing line is the best sentence either of us wrote. This one makes a claim instead: the constraint is misallocation, not scarcity. That matters because scarcity is a tragedy to be mourned and misallocation is a problem to be solved, and only one of those is fundable. It also names the three gates and the learning loop without listing them, and it survives being said aloud.

Attachment 1

Specific Aims

One page. It carries the whole argument, because a large share of reviewers form their judgment here and read the rest selectively.

Background. If we are fortunate, each of us will reach the point where we, or someone we love, needs help with the ordinary tasks of daily living. When that moment arrives, families meet a system that fails them at one of three gates. Can they find the care? Can they afford it? Is anyone available to deliver it? Failure at any one gate stops a recognized need from becoming established care.

All three gates are closing. Full-time home care runs $80,080 a year Assumed while an estimated $58 billion in assistance that could pay for it goes unclaimed annually Verified. Medicare does not cover custodial home care, and Medicaid, the nation's largest long-term care payer, pays only after a family has spent down. And 63.3 percent of home-care providers declined cases in 2023 that they could not staff Verified. Families of modest means therefore wait and decline until a hospitalization, when public programs finally arrive at the most expensive possible moment and after the most harm.

The consequences compound. Unmet needs with daily activities predict emergency visits, readmission, nursing-home placement, and mortality Verified, and each of those deepens the needs that went unmet. The trajectory worsens: 82 million Americans over 65 by 2050, against a family caregiver support ratio falling from more than seven potential caregivers per adult over 80 to four by 2030, and 9.7 million direct-care job openings this decade Verified.

Reviewer sound bite

The financing and delivery model for long-term care was built for a demographic structure that no longer exists.

What we built, and what remains. Across NIA SBIR Phases I through IIB (1R44AG074116, impact scores 20 and 25) Verified Olera built CareNavigator: an eldercare AI navigation system over an expert-curated national database of more than 72,000 aid programs and providers in all fifty states, with acceptance validated in four peer-reviewed studies Verified. The Phase IIB study section assessed the platform's commercial potential as extremely high Assumed. What SBIR did not buy, because it cannot be bought with research funds, is proof that a local market pays for itself.

Objective. We will bring Olera to full commercial operation in ten local markets, carrying families from recognized need to established aid and care, adding new caregivers to the providers who serve them, and finding the price at which those providers fund the platform. Families never pay, and no provider pays to be listed, Medicaid and Medicare included.

Aim 1. Establish care reliably, and prove it was established.

Can an agentic system carry a household from screened need to established aid or care, at a quality a licensed professional would approve, at a cost that falls?

Build and verify the execution and follow-up loops against blinded expert review; validate usability and trust with 25 family caregivers under IRB approval; measure cost to acquire and cost to serve from live records. Gate: expert-panel agreement 85 percent or higher, material errors under 10 percent.

Aim 2. Bring new caregivers into the workforce, and make providers want them.

Can we add caregivers who were not already in the field, make them employable through a verified record, and do they stay?

Verify the recruitment-to-placement pipeline end to end; validate acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility with providers and workers under IRB approval; measure net-new-worker share at intake and retention at 90 days and 12 months. Providers pay nothing in Aim 2. Gate: the staffing path completes end to end.

Aim 3. Determine whether providers pay, the economics hold, and the model replicates.

What will providers actually pay, do the unit economics work after real costs, and does the playbook repeat where it should struggle?

Randomized offer prices under real billing, starting from the prices providers already paid us; measure conversion, retention, churn, lifetime value against acquisition cost, payback, and per-market margin; independent analyst rebuilds the economics from billing records. Gate: LTV to CAC of 3:1 or better, payback under 12 months, positive per-market margin.

What exists at award end. A product that works, verified against expert review. A market model that repeats, run as written in market types chosen to include the ones where it should struggle. And an evidence package an outside analyst rebuilt from records, plus the linkage, cost model, and intermediate outcomes a risk-bearing payer requires before it will contract.

Reviewer sound bite

Nine years of federal investment bought the technology and the demand. It did not buy proof that a local market pays for itself, because that cannot be bought with research funds. It has to be measured in real markets, with real money changing hands.

Research Strategy  ·  Section 1

Significance

NIH requires this section to cover five things: the unmet need with its market segments and customers, how the project leads to a marketable product, the competitive environment, related development efforts in academia and industry, and hurdles to acceptance. It covers them in that order.

The problem is one problem with three gates

Care is difficult to find, difficult to afford, and increasingly impossible to staff. A family that clears two gates and fails the third ends up exactly where a family that cleared none does: with a need that never became care.

The trajectory, and who pays for it

This is not a steady-state problem. Demand is rising while both sources of supply contract. The informal capacity that has always absorbed most of American eldercare is halving per older adult, and the paid workforce that would have to replace it is short 9.7 million openings over the decade.

The costs of that gap do not vanish. They move. Function declines and emergency use rises. Working family caregivers, about 70 percent of the working-age caregiver population, lose hours and income Verified. Families spend down and Medicaid inherits the bill. The clearest measurement of the inversion comes from CAPABLE, a home-based function-focused intervention whose implementation was associated with roughly $3,000 of program cost against more than $20,000 in reduced Medicaid spending per participant Verified.

Reviewer sound bite

The money to support older adults at home largely already exists. It is simply spent later, on worse outcomes, by whoever is left holding the consequence. Every dollar of that consequence sits on an identifiable balance sheet, and that is what makes this a market and not only a tragedy.

Why navigation alone is insufficient

A navigation product that clears gates one and two aggregates family demand and delivers it to a supply side that cannot absorb it. The result is a faster path to the same disappointment. This is the reason workforce expansion is inside our product rather than beside it: we did not choose staffing as an adjacent business, we encountered it as the binding constraint on our own product working.

Established care therefore requires two things at once. Navigation that runs all the way to completion, and capacity that exists when it arrives. That combination is what our product is.

Who pays, and why

Families never pay. Revenue comes from the side of the market with a budget and a bleeding constraint. During this award we sell exactly one product, Caregiver Staffing, and everything else on the table below is a measurement obligation rather than a sales target.

Related development efforts in academia and industry

Most of the relevant science is settled, and what remains open is operational. That distinction is the reason this is a commercialization award and not a trial.

  • Unmet need research (NHATS, Medicare-linked cohorts) establishes that unmet daily-activity needs predict emergency use, readmission, placement, and mortality. We take this as settled and measure care established, not clinical endpoints.
  • Home-based support trials (CAPABLE, Johns Hopkins) show function-focused home support reduces disability with Medicaid savings well above program cost. What no trial addresses is how a family outside a trial finds such support, funds it, and gets it started. CareNavigator is the front door those programs assume already exists.
  • Digital caregiver tools and AI, including four peer-reviewed studies of our own, establish that caregiver platforms can be built and measured as usable and accepted. What remains open is whether agents can complete applications and follow a case to a decision.
  • Payer-funded navigation is no longer hypothetical. The Medicare GUIDE model began in July 2024 and pays participants for care navigation, caregiver support, and respite in dementia Verified. The open question is whether navigation beyond dementia is paid the same way.
  • Health-professions pipeline research establishes that admission to these programs requires documented patient-care hours. Whether that requirement converts into durable eldercare capacity is what Aim 2 tests.

Hurdles to adoption

The primary hurdle is local density at a cost the market sustains. Families, providers, and caregivers must concentrate in the same market, and that market's revenue must cover the cost of bringing them together. Four specific hurdles follow, each with its mitigation and the aim that tests it: families must trust AI with care decisions (answers come from an expert-curated database audited by domain experts; Aim 1 validates acceptance under IRB); providers are wary of platforms (they start free and pay only after value is delivered; Aim 3 measures conversion and churn under real billing); new caregivers must be safe and welcome (licensed providers hire, train, insure, and supervise them as employees; Aim 2 measures the pathway end to end); and every market must repeat affordably (self-service portals and a documented playbook; Aims 1 and 2 measure acquisition cost per market).

Research Strategy  ·  Section 2

Innovation

Three departures. The first is the one no competitor can copy by building software, and it leads because it is the most consequential.

1. Capacity created, not moved

Every staffing channel that serves this industry, agencies, job boards, and gig platforms alike, competes for workers already in the direct-care market. One provider's hire is another provider's vacancy. The national shortage does not shrink by a single worker.

Olera adds people who were not in the field. The first population tested is health-professions students, chosen because admission to their programs requires documented patient-care hours, which makes recruitment structurally cheap: 263,800 health-professions degrees are conferred annually Verified and our pilot drew more than 900 applicants Verified. New cohorts arrive every semester, so the pipeline refills rather than depleting.

But the innovation is not the student channel, which is copyable. It is the infrastructure underneath: the machinery that turns a person who has never worked in eldercare into someone a licensed provider will hire, and that carries their verified record of hours, competencies, populations served, supervisor evaluations, and credential status forward across employers. That layer is what makes any new worker pool viable, and it compounds with every shift worked. A Year 3 feasibility pilot in two campus-poor markets tests a second, non-student pool against the same pathway.

Reviewer sound bite

Every other channel in this industry competes for the same overextended workers. This one adds workers. That is the difference between redistributing a shortage and reducing it.

2. Execution, not information

The paradigm today is that tools inform and the family executes. A family is told which programs they may qualify for, and is then left to prepare applications, gather documents, file, wait, follow up, and confirm. They are lost at exactly those steps. Our agents do the work and the family decides, which changes the unit of success from information delivered to care established. Every completed case becomes training data for the next one.

3. A record of what actually happens, which no public dataset contains

This is the innovation we have been under-claiming. Three layers of information exist. The open web holds public directories and program rules, often neither accurate nor current, and it is available to any general-purpose AI. Beneath that sit local program realities and provider truths that were never published, which we assembled by field work and expert curation into more than 72,000 records and 700 guides Verified. Beneath that sits a third layer that can only be learned from completed cases: what programs actually decide, county by county, where applications stall, whether capacity existed, and what care started.

No public dataset holds the third layer. NHATS is a survey. Claims data show what was billed, not what was needed and denied. What this project builds is the first longitudinal, county-level administrative record of what older adults need, what they qualify for, where the system fails them, and whether care was established.

Reviewer sound bite

A competitor who copies every feature starts with an empty database. More importantly, so does every researcher and every state agency, because this measurement does not currently exist anywhere.

Note for Logan. This reframe is new and it is the boldest move in the package. It converts our weakest-scoring innovation into a scientific contribution NIA can value on its own terms, and it supplies the "societal, educational, and scientific benefits" the Commercialization Plan is required to describe. It also creates an obligation we should accept deliberately: if we claim it is measurement infrastructure, we should commit to publishing from it.

Research Strategy  ·  Section 3

Approach

If someone gave us $4 million to determine whether this should become national care infrastructure, this is the experiment we would run.

The one uncertainty that is left

Two NIH awards and our own capital retired five commercial risks (Section 4). One remains, and it is the one research funding cannot buy: can a single local market pay for itself, and can we repeat it? Three aims answer that as one chain of evidence.

Market design, derived rather than chosen

We reopened this question from first principles rather than carrying forward a number. Two findings changed the answer.

First, the statistical requirement was overstated roughly threefold. The prior derivation called for 300 paying accounts. A price experiment randomizes accounts that receive an offer; conversion is the outcome. At an expected 35 percent conversion, 180 offers yields about 63 paying accounts. Second, we do not need a hypothesis test. A business must choose a price under a pre-registered rule, which is estimation, not null rejection. Estimating conversion to a 95 percent interval of plus or minus 12 points at three price points needs about 180 offers total, and an adaptive design that drops a dominated arm at interim needs fewer.

Statistics is therefore not the binding constraint. Replication is.

The uncertainty that could kill the thesis, named out loud

If new-entrant caregivers do not stay long enough to be worth more to a provider than they cost to acquire, providers will not pay twice, and the commercial model fails regardless of how well navigation performs. That is the single greatest risk in this project, it is why Aim 2 measures retention at both 90 days and 12 months, and it is why Aim 2 runs from Year 1 rather than waiting for Aim 1 to finish.

Stop rule. If at month 24 fewer than 40 percent of placed workers remain in direct care at 90 days and paid conversion is below 20 percent at every offered price, we will report the provider-funded model as disconfirmed, halt market expansion, and redirect remaining effort to completing the payer evidence package. We would rather publish that result than spend Year 3 defending it.

What this award proves, and what it only prepares

A risk-bearing organization needs six things before it will contract. This award delivers five and refuses the sixth, and the refusal is what makes the first five credible.

Human subjects, and the clinical-trial line

Three bounded IRB studies, one per aim, conducted through Clemson: family-caregiver usability and trust (Aim 1, n=25); provider and worker acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility (Aim 2); and a small qualitative adoption and price-fairness component (Aim 3). Everything else is operational and commercial evidence from platform, billing, and employment records.

The project stays outside the NIH clinical-trial definition by design. No one is prospectively assigned to receive or not receive navigation. Health-related outcomes appear only as secondary observational measures on a cohort that all receives the service. Price arms are assigned to business accounts and the outcome is a purchase, not a health outcome. Worker-facing research is firewalled from employment decisions in the protocol. Determinations are obtained rather than assumed, including for the streams we expect to be non-human-subjects research, and every determination is listed so a reviewer does not have to guess.

Regulatory plan

No FDA pathway applies. The product is navigational and administrative software that does not diagnose, treat, or make clinical decisions, so it is not a medical device and no premarket authorization gates commercialization. What does govern is addressed explicitly: HIPAA-aligned privacy safeguards; marketing and referral practice, where charging no fee for a referral means no steering incentive exists; employment and supervision, where licensed providers employ, train, insure, and supervise every worker; and payer linkage, which requires a data-use agreement and an IRB determination before any record is joined.

Technical assistance and oversight

Three external providers, each with defined deliverables and integration mechanics. Clemson University (Qiping Fan, PhD, Co-I at quarter time) designs and executes the three IRB studies and holds the human-subjects protocols. An independent financial analyst, engaged in Year 3, rebuilds the unit economics from raw billing and cost records without access to our model, which is the difference between an audited number and a claimed one. Actuarial consultation supports the payer cost model. Oversight runs through a weekly operating review and a quarterly milestone review against the gates in Table 5, with the PI accountable for integration and go/no-go decisions.

Attachment 3

Commercialization Plan

Eleven sections in the order the SF424 and the NOFO jointly require. Compressed here to the answers, not the prose.

1. Statement of Need

The Valley of Death. Olera has a validated technology and demonstrated demand, and no proof that a market pays for itself. Investors price that gap as unfinanceable: the evidence they require is generated only by operating at commercial scale, and operating at commercial scale requires the capital they will not commit until the evidence exists.

Why federal funding, specifically. Three activities in this plan would not otherwise happen. Running a price experiment that can fail, and publishing the result, is something no venture-backed company does. Concentrating families, providers, and a new workforce in markets deliberately chosen to include the ones where the model should struggle is the opposite of what growth capital funds. And building the payer evidence package for products that generate no revenue during the award is a three-year investment with no near-term return.

Reviewer sound bite

Private capital would fund us to grow where it already works. Only federal funding will pay us to find out where it does not.

2. Value of the Project, Expected Outcomes, and Impact

The product is CareNavigator plus the workforce infrastructure that makes its output deliverable. Current approaches fail because each solves one stretch of the path and stops at a handoff. Commercially, the award produces a validated provider-funded revenue model and an investor-ready evidence package. Non-commercially, it produces the first county-level longitudinal record of eldercare need and resolution, which we commit to publishing from, and a pathway that brings new workers into a field short 9.7 million openings while giving health-professions students the documented patient-care hours their training requires.

3. Company

Olera, Inc. is a fully virtual company: two full-time and two part-time employees, engineering and marketing support, a two-person call center, and contract staff Verified. Being virtual is an operating advantage for a geographically distributed project, and it is why market entry costs what it does. Funding history is approximately $2.7M in NIA SBIR awards. There is no recurring commercial revenue today and no venture capital raised. Management scales with the award into commercial operations, market management, and workforce operations roles.

4. SBIR/STTR Commercialization History

No name changes, not a subsidiary or spin-off. Substantially all revenue in each of the past five years derived from SBIR funding, reported year by year Assumed. Two Phase II awards. Revenue generated to date from commercialization of SBIR-funded projects is limited to paid staffing pilots. We state this plainly rather than dressing it: the prior awards were research awards, the technology they produced works and has been independently praised for its commercial potential, and this application exists precisely because converting that technology into revenue is the step that has not yet been funded.

Synthesis

Why NIH should fund this now

America is not short of the resources to care for its aging population. It is short of the machinery to release them. Fifty-eight billion dollars of assistance goes unclaimed each year while families spend down to Medicaid. Hundreds of thousands of people would enter caregiving if a path existed, while providers turn away two of every three cases they cannot staff. Both failures produce the same outcome: a recognized need that never becomes established care, and a family that ends up in the hospital instead.

Nine years of NIA investment built the technology that closes the first gap and validated that families accept it. Our own capital, deployed beyond the Phase II scope, showed that families arrive by the thousand at almost no acquisition cost, that providers show up, and that providers will pay for new caregivers. Every risk that research money can retire has been retired.

One is left. Whether a local market pays for itself, and whether that repeats. It cannot be answered by more research, because it is only answerable with real customers paying real prices over a real retention window. And it cannot be answered by private capital, because private capital funds expansion where the model already works and will not fund the experiment that determines whether it does, least of all in the markets where it might not.

That is precisely the gap this program exists to close. Three years and $4 million buys a definitive answer: a product verified against expert review, a price chosen by experiment rather than assumption, unit economics an outside analyst rebuilt from records, a playbook run as written in ten markets across four market types, and the linkage and cost model that let institutional payers begin their own validation the day the award ends.

If the answer is yes, NIH will have converted its own nine-year investment into national care infrastructure that runs on private revenue and stays free for families forever. If the answer is no, we will have determined that at a fraction of what the market would spend discovering it, and we will publish it. Either outcome is worth knowing, and only one funder will pay to find out.

My read

A 24 is fundable at NIA in most rounds and is a genuine improvement on the low-to-mid 30s the current materials would earn. It is also short of the 10 to 20 you asked me to reach for, and I do not think the gap is a writing problem. Three things cap it: no prior commercialization, no IP, and a $4 million ask against a ten-county end state. The first two cannot be fixed before September 4. The third is a design choice we could still revisit.

The most useful thing this exercise revealed is that the harshest critic's first paragraph is answerable and we have not answered it. The counter is that the deliverable is a validated, transferable market model plus the payer evidence package, and that the ten counties are an instrument rather than the outcome. That argument exists in our thinking and appears nowhere on the page as a direct rebuttal. Making it explicit is worth more than any other single edit available to us.
