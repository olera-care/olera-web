B. ACCOMPLISHMENTS

B.1 WHAT ARE THE MAJOR GOALS OF THE PROJECT?

The US aging care system is fragmented, expensive, and difficult to navigate for families with an elder loved one in need of care support. This project aims to address the high costs and accessibility barriers in U.S. senior care by developing and evaluating an AI system and experience for older adults and family caregivers, particularly those with dementia, that will match individuals with eligible financial aid programs to pay for basic needs (health care, food, rent, etc.) and support systems for home or facility-based services. It builds upon our previous Fast-Track SBIR project by leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) and generative AI to enhance senior care planning and care coordination, ultimately aimed at improving the quality of life of aging Americans, and avoiding costly emergencies for families and Medicare from elder neglect in under-resourced situations.

B.5 HOW HAVE THE RESULTS BEEN DISSEMINATED TO COMMUNITIES OF INTEREST?

The project's research is directly motivated by the unmet needs of AD/ADRD family caregivers and focuses especially on those in under-resourced households who are typically outside the reach of academic research dissemination channels. During this reporting period, the team continued to engage these communities through the project's established network of 10,000+ family caregivers and older adults (Facebook and Aging in America YouTube Channel), our formal research study, and through ongoing relationships with Alzheimer's Association chapters, Area Agencies on Aging (AAAs) caregiver networks, and aging-services organizations, including home care and senior living facilities. These engagements have led to the dissemination of key insights to help families understand financial aid and benefits eligibility (i.e., SNAP, Medicaid HCBC waivers, VA Aid & Attendance, among others), HCBC service matching and costs, and care coordination best-practices, delivering information directly to caregiver communities through out web-based platform, articles, and recruitment for studies. Many outreach efforts reach families that would not otherwise encounter this information through scientific channels.

Additionally, the project's research findings on AI-enabled needs assessment, LTSS matching, and benefits/aid eligibility guidance are translated into a public-facing intelligent care navigation experience that families can access directly. Through this channel, research-informed information reaches approximately 25,000–30,000 family caregivers (our monthly website visits per month). This represents a meaningful translation of the project's insights into a form usable by members of the caregiving public, many of whom face the financial, informational, and logistical barriers that motivated this award and would not otherwise be reached

B.2 — What was accomplished under these goals?

During this reporting period (June 1, 2025 – May 31, 2026), the project advanced from agent development into full multi-agent system integration paired with an intelligent user interface, completed preliminary usability testing with AD/ADRD family caregivers (n=25), and substantially expanded the supporting elder care resource infrastructure. Aim 1 was further refined, Aim 2 was completed end-to-end, and the team is positioned to launch the Aim 3 diverse-cohort study in the upcoming award year (June 1, 2026 – May 31, 2027).

Specific Objectives (Aims/Milestones) Pursued This Period. (1) Aim 1: Refine the specialized AI agents (Needs Assessment Agent and unified Resource Matching/Care Planning Agent) based on expert feedback. (2) Aim 2: Complete integration of the Multi-Agent Network with an intelligent user interface and conduct preliminary usability testing with n=25 AD/ADRD family caregivers. (3) Cross-cutting: Expand the Educational Materials and Caregiver Resources (EMCR) database to broaden the data substrate underlying the Resource Matching agent and prepare for Aim 3.

Major Activities. Aim 1 — Expert-informed refinement. Licensed clinicians and internal subject-matter experts on the elder care ecosystem reviewed agent outputs across representative caregiver scenarios and provided structured feedback used to refine intake and recommendations. Refinement targeted three priorities: (i) more accurate matching of care seekers to local long-term services and supports (LTSS), including home care agencies, home health, assisted living facilities, nursing homes, rehabilitation centers, hospice agencies, and adult day care centers; (ii) better matching to wraparound supports such as food assistance, transportation, technology assistance, and other community-based programs that fill gaps LTSS providers cannot or that offset out-of-pocket costs; and (iii) improved guidance on federal, state, and local benefits and aid programs, calibrated to the user's care needs and the recipient's stage of cognitive or physical decline. Matching logic was refined to better differentiate users likely to qualify for Medicaid-linked support from users who do not qualify but still need affordability pathways, and the system now consistently directs non-qualifying families to their local Area Agency on Aging (AAA) as a fallback. Aim 2 — Integration and pilot testing. The eldercare-AI Network was fully integrated with a caregiver-facing intelligent user interface that included a conversational interface through which families interact through voice-to-voice conversation, with additional support for text-based input and text-to-voice output. The integrated system was evaluated in the planned n=25 pilot. Caregivers provided data and feedback on the experience through the User Experience Evaluation in Intelligent Environments (UXIE) framework, the Mobile Application Rating Scale (MARS), the modified Technology Acceptance Survey (TAS), and follow-up qualitative interviews. Cross-cutting effort — EMCR expansion. The repository was expanded across both LTSS provider categories and federal, state, and local benefits and aid programs, including SNAP, PACE, VA Aid and Attendance, Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers, Medicaid waivers supporting assisted living, LIHEAP, Medicare Savings Programs, AAAs, and additional community-based and nonprofit programs, among others. The team also developed provider- and program-manager-facing workflows allowing operators to update and validate their own information over time, paired with human fallback pathways through which families can ask follow-up questions. Cross-cutting effort — IRB amendment. The team initiated an IRB amendment with Clemson University to update the research protocol for our next approved Aim 3 study (n=200) based on Aim 2 learnings before enrollment begins.

Significant Results, Findings, and Developments. Aim 1 (positive): The refined agent system reliably handles the priority matching scenarios and consistently routes non-qualifying families to AAA fallback pathways. Aim 2 (positive): the AI Agent Network was successfully integrated with a working voice-to-voice intelligent UI and tested end-to-end with n=25 AD/ADRD caregivers, validating the feasibility of deploying a conversational-based AI care navigation experience to this population in a real-use context. Aim 2 continued): caregivers responded differently to different avatar styles, voice modes, and interaction formats. This finding directly informs the next engineering cycle: the team is preparing a more flexible, multimodal next version of the interface that includes expanded support for varied avatar styles, voice modes, and interaction pathways to be built during Q2 2026 in advance of Aim 3. EMCR (positive): the repository grew from approximately 45,000 to approximately 70,000 indexed entities during this period (\~56% growth), with a near-term target of greater than or equal to 100,000 LTSS providers and benefits program. This EMCR now spans both LTSS and a broad set of benefits and aid programs. The provider/program-manager validation workflows and human fallback pathways represent the team's emerging solution to one of the long-standing challenges of online indexed directories for care resources surrounding data accuracy and freshness, and lay the groundwork for sustaining EMCR quality at the scale required for Aim 3 and Phase III commercialization.

E.2 WHAT IS THE IMPACT ON PHYSICAL, INSTITUTIONAL, OR INFORMATION RESOURCES THAT FORM INFRASTRUCTURE?

The project is contributing to the development of a nationwide digital infrastructure for elder care navigation. The Educational Materials and Caregiver Resources (EMCR) database now contains approximately 70,000 indexed long-term services and supports providers and benefit programs and is externally accessible to families through the Olera platform. This database and the interface for it on the web platform are positioned to become one of the most comprehensive structured indexes of the U.S. elder care ecosystem available to the public. Coupled with the AI matching framework and caregiver- and provider-facing interface, this project represents an emerging information-resource data infrastructure that helps families identify local services and financial programs aligned with their care needs and surface affordability pathways that can offset out-of-pocket costs. As the EMCR continues to expand toward our next goal of 100,000 indexed entities, its value as a public information resource will continue to grow.

E.3 WHAT IS THE IMPACT ON TECHNOLOGY TRANSFER?

The project achieved meaningful technology transfer and commercialization momentum during this reporting period.

Customer discovery and ecosystem engagement. The team conducted over 300–400 B2B conversations with health systems, private and public elder care providers, Alzheimer's Association chapters, Area Agencies on Aging, and other elder-care ecosystem stakeholders. These conversations were conducted according to standard customer discovery best practices informed by prior NIH/NSF Innovation-Corps (I-Corps) training. As a result, they have directly shaped the development of the provider-facing portal, which is designed by user request to allow providers to update their profiles detailing their services, accurately represent their service categories, receive interest from care seekers in the form of qualifying data payloads, and (in development) manage intake and conversion workflows.

Pilot adoption and traction. Several pilot customers are actively engaged with the platform. The platform supports over 1,000 provider users and approximately 25,000–30,000 family care-seeker visits per month. This traction has been driven primarily by organic search, SEO, AI search optimization, and other non-paid channels, which indicate durable demand for the kind of care navigation the project enables.

Commercialization model. The team's commercialization philosophy is to keep the platform free for families and care seekers while monetizing provider-facing value-added tools in a non-extractive way. We are preparing for Phase III commercialization by exploring commercialization directions that include premium provider subscriptions for review generation, practice family outreach tools, profile and intake optimization tools, qualified-lead access, hiring platform functionality to address workforce shortages, and transaction facilitation for services such as home care, assisted living, and third-party payer workflows. These directions remain in commercialization planning rather than active revenue lines at this time, with plans for full-on commercialization in Phase III.

Potential participants with children under 18 years of age will be excluded from participating in the research because we believe the children are usually not actively involved in the financial and legal decisions of PLwD. Also, the overarching project focuses on the family caregiver population who are actively in financial management and legal decisions of PLwD, therefore, we believe the exclusion of children under 18 is scientifically justified.

Exclusion of Prisoners This study requires human subjects to either be living in a community-dwelling residence or assisted living facility or providing care to a senior with AD/ADRD at a home or assisted living facility. Therefore, prisoners are excluded from this study.

3.3 Data and Safety Monitoring Plan

The conduct of this study and associated data and safety monitoring plan will adhere to the protocol that will be approved by the research team and the Clemson University Institutional Review Board (IRB). The data will include: survey data, audio-record data, interview transcripts, platform-recorded user data, and observation notes which will be kept on secure servers that are password protected.

In addition, participant's name and contact information (e.g., phone and email) will be collected to enable follow-up with participants. These administrative data will be kept separate from the study data and linked via unique participant identifier. Access to the identifiable information and link to the coded data will be restricted to the authorized data users. All of the materials collected are for research purposes only, and data will be kept in strict confidence. The platform data will be handled in a secure database with administration and user access. Data collected via Olera collected is stored securely in the platform-managed database, and are only accessible by platform manager. Our application will require robust passwords to login.

For the proposed project, we expect minimal risks for participants. Any harm experienced by a subject that is unexpected and information that indicates a new or increased risk including unanticipated problems will be documented and reported to the IRB and/or other agencies as appropriate. Key investigators involved in the human subject research will collectively review the details regarding the adverse events, modify the protocol accordingly, and seek approval of the change by the Clemson IRB (as appropriate).

As a part of data quality management, the PIs or designated data coordinator will review data collection to ensure data completeness per occurrence (for surveys) and back-up data monthly. The datasets will be stored in an encrypted external hard drive in a locked location, as well as, in an encrypted online cloud and will be only accessible by the authorized personnel. Lastly, The Advisory Board will meet twelve times for a total of approximately 24 hours during the 36 months of the study to review data, adjust measurement criteria, and review processes and outcomes.
