## RESUME AND SUMMARY OF DISCUSSION

This CRP application proposes to extend a mature, NIA-funded eldercare navigation platform (CareNavigator) from *planning* into *execution*, and to pair it with a provider-facing workforce product (Caregiver Staffing) that supplies new caregivers to the local labor market. Three aims are proposed in a strictly gated sequence: Aim 1 engineers and technically verifies a longitudinal Care Establishment Model, bounded AI agent execution, a field-learning knowledge layer, and staffing infrastructure, and validates usability and trust with 75 stakeholders before any real-world action; Aim 2 moves the verified automation envelope into live markets and measures whether an index family episode reaches verified aid or care establishment within 90 days; Aim 3 introduces price and tests whether demonstrated value converts into paid conversion, retention, unit economics, and a repeatable market-entry playbook, and assembles an institutional-buyer evidence package.

Enthusiasm centered on four points that reviewers regarded as genuine and well argued. First, the Significance section is among the clearest customer-definition sections the panel would see in this round: it separates user (family, free), participant (provider, free), and buyer (provider for staffing; risk-bearing institutions, evidence-gated) without blurring them, and it is explicit that the second market is gated on evidence the applicant does not yet have. Second, the central intellectual claim — that the eldercare care-establishment pathway must be made *computable* before it can be reliably automated or measured, and that executed cases produce local operational truth unavailable on the public web — is a substantive and non-obvious thesis rather than an assertion that AI is innovative. Third, the risk-retirement narrative in the Progress Report is cumulative and unusually disciplined: each prior stage tested one uncertainty before the next layer was built, and the applicant is candid that manual delivery of Caregiver Staffing was deliberate learning rather than product. Fourth, the gating architecture — technical verification before human exposure, human-protection overrides, real-world value before price, and an explicit willingness to publish a negative result and stop rather than scale a failing model — is exactly the posture a CRP should exhibit, and several reviewers described it as the strongest structural feature of the application.

Concerns were substantial and concentrated in Approach and in the arithmetic linking the two documents. The most serious is that the application's gates are structurally excellent but numerically empty at precisely the decision points that matter: Aim 1's Care Establishment Model criterion is "prespecified completeness" with no threshold; Aim 2's primary criterion is a day-90 establishment proportion "estimated with prespecified precision" against a success criterion that is never stated, and an N of ~400 that is explicitly deferred to a power analysis not present in the application. A GO/NO-GO gate whose threshold will be set after the application is reviewed is not a gate that reviewers can score. Second, Aim 2's primary endpoint is agnostic to whether the innovation performed the work: a family confirming that care was established is fully consistent with Olera's two existing full-time call-center staff and human navigators executing every consequential step, and no endpoint, criterion, or prespecified target separates agent-executed from human-executed pathways. Third, the workforce revenue model rests on a throughput assumption ("10 successful hires per county per month," described as conservative) that is roughly twenty times the company's entire lifetime placement history and is supported by no data in either document; at the applicant's own reported pilot yield the Year 3 revenue figure requires on the order of seventeen thousand applicants in six months. Fourth, the two documents disagree materially on the number of markets — the Research Strategy's Progress Report says two, the Commercialization Plan says approximately eight and builds its entire revenue model on eight, and Aim 2 says the number will be fixed later — and because market count, sample size, revenue, and operating cost are all functions of one another, this single discrepancy destabilizes the feasibility case in both directions. Fifth, Aim 2 is single-arm with no concurrent comparator, so the institutional value proposition on which the Commercialization Plan's Year 4 and Year 5 revenue depends cannot be causally supported by the evidence the Research Strategy is designed to produce; Aim 3, Task 3.4 in fact delivers a *design* for a future payer study rather than payer-grade evidence, while the Commercialization Plan books a $250K institutional contract twelve months after the award ends. Reviewers also noted a weak formal IP position (trade secret and copyright only; patents merely "will evaluate"), a Fundraising Plan resting on relationships the applicant itself twice describes as not being commitments, and a very lean operating team — one full-time engineer and two full-time call-center staff — for a program that must build a longitudinal state model, agentic execution with browser automation and fax, a field-learning layer, and staffing infrastructure in Year 1 alone.

Disagreement was expected on two axes. Some reviewers would score Significance a 3 rather than a 2, on the grounds that the SBIR Significance criterion explicitly folds in whether the Commercialization Plan demonstrates a *high probability* of commercialization, and that probability is undercut by unvalidated pricing, a nil commercialization track record across ~$5.7M of prior federal support, and no private capital history. Others would hold Significance at 2 and locate those concerns entirely in Environment and in the Commercialization Plan assessment. A second disagreement concerns whether the unspecified Aim 2 threshold is a fixable drafting defect or a substantive weakness; the majority view in this simulated panel was that because the same document specifies thresholds precisely elsewhere (SUS ≥72, TIAS ≥5, ≥90% action agreement, ≥50% placement), the absence of a threshold at the single most consequential gate reads as unresolved rather than merely unwritten.

On balance, the panel judged that the significance of the problem, the coherence of the technical thesis, the discipline of the risk sequencing, and the team's demonstrated delivery against two prior NIA awards outweigh the identified weaknesses, but not decisively. The weaknesses are specific, quantitative, and concentrated in the Approach and in the commercial arithmetic, and several are correctable. Impact is likely to be very good.

## CRITIQUE 1 — INTEGRATED PRIMARY REVIEWER

**Significance: 2 · Investigator(s): 3 · Innovation: 3 · Approach: 5 · Environment: 4**

### Overall Impact

This application proposes the right project for the CRP mechanism: it is not further discovery, and it is not a request to fund scale-up of an already-proven business. It sits precisely in the gap the mechanism exists to bridge — a nationally deployed, peer-reviewed, demonstrably used platform that has never been shown to *complete* the job it starts, run by a company with essentially no commercial revenue after ~$5.7M of federal support. The applicant's framing of that gap is honest and the three-aim sequence maps onto it cleanly: build and verify, then prove real-world value, then test whether value converts to money. The intellectual core — that eldercare cannot be automated until the pathway is represented as computable longitudinal state, and that executing cases generates local operational truth that no directory or claims file contains — is a real contribution and the strongest reason to fund this work.

The application's principal limitation is that it is more rigorous about *architecture* than about *numbers*. The gating logic is superb; the thresholds behind two of the three gates are absent or unjustified. The instrumentation plan is comprehensive; the primary endpoint does not distinguish the intervention from the incumbent human process it is meant to replace. The revenue model is arithmetically self-consistent and unusually candid in labeling hypotheses as hypotheses; the single throughput input that drives it is ~20× the company's demonstrated performance and is called conservative. The Commercialization Plan needs causal evidence of avoided utilization to justify its institutional revenue; the Research Strategy explicitly and correctly declines to produce it. These are not fatal, but they are the difference between an application a panel funds enthusiastically and one it funds with reservations.

Score-driving contributors, positive: an exceptionally well-specified unmet need with a clean user/participant/buyer separation; a genuine and defensible technical thesis in the Care Establishment Model; a disciplined, honest, cumulative risk-retirement history including real (if tiny) revenue and 900 workforce applicants; and a gated design with human protection as an explicit override. Score-driving contributors, negative: unspecified success criteria at the Aim 2 gate and no power justification for N≈400; a primary endpoint blind to automation share; a workforce throughput assumption unsupported by and contradicted by the applicant's own pilot yield; a market count that differs materially between the two documents; a single-arm design that cannot support the institutional value claim the Commercialization Plan monetizes; and a very lean team relative to the Year 1 engineering load.

### 1. Significance — Score: 2

**Strengths**
- The unmet need is established with specificity and appropriate citation: $80,080 annual full-time home care cost, Medicare's exclusion of custodial care, ~$58B in unclaimed aid, 63.3% of home-care providers declining cases for lack of staff in 2023, and the demographic scissors of 82M over-65s by 2050 against family caregivers per adult over 80 falling from >7 to 4.
- The link from unmet need to health consequence is made with real evidence rather than assertion: unmet ADL needs independently predicting ED use, readmission, nursing-home placement, and mortality, and the Commercialization Plan's HCBS figures (52% vs. 34% ED use; 36% vs. 24% hospital/rehab stays).
- The customer architecture is unusually clear and is consistent across both documents. Families are free users; providers are free network participants *and* paying staffing customers; risk-bearing institutions are explicitly labeled an evidence-gated market. Very few applications of this type keep user, participant, and buyer this cleanly separated.
- The applicant states plainly that the institutional market is *not yet purchasable* and that the CRP must generate the evidence to open it. This is the correct posture and reviewers credited it.
- The competitive analysis (Research Strategy Table 2) is honest and structurally intelligent: alternatives are mapped against the seven steps of the care-establishment pathway rather than against a vague category, and Olera is given full marks only in the "after the CRP" row. Named competitors include the ones that actually matter (Wellthy, Homethrive, Cariloop, A Place for Mom, Caring.com, CareYaya, Indeed, and general-purpose AI).
- Hurdles to adoption are named for each of the four constituencies rather than generically, and each is mapped to a CRP activity (Commercialization Plan Table 5).
- The GUIDE Model is correctly deployed: not as proof that Olera will be paid, but as precedent that CMS is already paying for navigation and caregiver support with the explicit aim of delaying institutionalization.

**Weaknesses**
- The beachhead market is never sized in dollars. The application gives worker counts (4.68M aides), openings (760,500/yr), and turnover (75%), but never the addressable *spend* on caregiver recruitment that Caregiver Staffing competes for. Without it, reviewers cannot judge whether a $250-per-hire product addresses a large market or a thin one.
- The application's own scale illustration argues against it. At 500 counties, 60,000 successful hires — approximately 8% of all annual U.S. direct-care openings — yields only $15M in revenue. Capturing 8% of a national labor market for $15M suggests the price point, the value capture, or the market framing is mismatched, and reviewers will notice that the illustration meant to demonstrate headroom instead demonstrates a low revenue ceiling per unit of operational effort.
- The strategic coherence of the two products is asserted more strongly than the Commercialization Plan supports. The Research Strategy argues Caregiver Staffing exists because capacity is required to establish care; the Commercialization Plan concedes that staffing revenue arises "regardless of where the underlying client originates... existing clients, externally generated referrals, growth, turnover replacement, or a capacity constraint observed through CareNavigator." On the applicant's own account, most staffing revenue will have nothing to do with CareNavigator, which makes the beachhead substantially a separate recruitment business rather than an integrated one.
- Willingness-to-pay evidence for the beachhead is three providers. Three is enough to establish that the number is not zero; it is not enough to establish a price, a package, or a demand curve, and the Significance case leans on it.
- Under the SBIR Significance criterion, the probability of commercialization is itself scored. That probability is materially reduced by a nil commercialization record across two prior Phase II awards, an IP position limited to trade secret and copyright, and a financing plan resting on non-commitments — and reviewers will import some of that into this score.

### 3. Innovation — Score: 3

**Strengths**
- The framing avoids the most common failure mode in this space: the application does not claim novelty because it uses LLMs. It claims novelty in a *representation* — a longitudinal Care Establishment Model over seven eldercare domains carrying substates, timestamped events, geography, provenance, evidence, ownership of the next action, and explicit terminal states. That is a defensible and non-obvious engineering thesis, and the argument for why it is load-bearing ("software cannot reliably automate a fragmented pathway unless it can observe what state a case is in, what changed, who owns the next action, and what evidence defines completion") is the best paragraph in the application.
- The measurement corollary is genuinely novel: directories describe what should exist and claims describe what was billed, but executed cases reveal what actually happened between recognized need and established care. Framing execution as an instrument rather than only as a service is a real contribution.
- The field-learning concept is the most distinctive idea in the package and is well illustrated: a provider publicly accepts a payer but reports by phone that weekend capacity is unavailable until November; that fact improves the current case and every subsequent case in that market. The observation that eldercare operational truth is local, fast-changing, and largely absent from the public web is correct and is a real basis for a compounding data asset.
- The bounded-execution loop (interpret, plan, permission, act, observe, update, wait or return, verify or escalate) with the explicit principle that "sending a form or leaving a voicemail is an event, not an outcome" is a mature and appropriately unglamorous statement of what the hard part actually is.
- Human-control boundaries are drawn as design constraints rather than disclaimers: attestation, legal authority, submission, scheduling, and consequential family choice remain human; the system does not diagnose, prescribe, place orders, or make clinical decisions.

**Weaknesses**
- The agent execution layer is the least defensible of the three innovations on a commercial time horizon. General-purpose agentic execution — browser automation, document handling, scheduling, voice — is being commoditized rapidly by platform vendors; the durable novelty is the domain state model and the local operational corpus, not the agents. The application does not distinguish which of its advantages survive a general-purpose agent becoming competent at portal navigation, and reviewers familiar with the trajectory will ask.
- Innovation 3 is materially weaker than the other two. Recruiting pre-health students into paid caregiving is not novel — the applicant's own Commercialization Plan names CareYaya as an existing student-caregiver model. The claimed innovation is the *data-directed targeting* of new supply at observed capacity deficits, but no part of the Approach tests whether deficit-targeted recruitment outperforms untargeted recruitment. Without that comparison, the innovation reduces to conventional student recruitment plus a dashboard.
- The claim that the architecture is "labor-pool agnostic" is asserted and never tested. Only one pool (pre-health students) is used, and the Approach contains no task that would demonstrate transfer to a second pool.
- Field learning has no accuracy criterion anywhere in the application (see Approach). An innovation whose stated value is knowledge quality is measured only on process compliance.
- Nothing in the Innovation section is protected by anything stronger than trade secret, and the Commercialization Plan defers patent evaluation entirely. For innovations described as load-bearing and compounding, that is a thin position.

### 4. Approach — Score: 5

**Strengths**
- The staged architecture is the application's best feature and is correctly ordered: technical verification precedes human exposure, real-world value precedes price, and payment does not relax any human-protection boundary established earlier. Aim 1 gates Aim 2; Aim 2 gates Aim 3. This is exactly right for a system that takes consequential actions.
- The primary Aim 2 endpoint is well chosen and is the hardest available: the family must confirm that the primary aid or care need documented at intake was actually obtained within 90 days, with source verification where available, and the application states explicitly that "a referral, application, benefit approval, provider match, scheduled appointment, or job offer alone is not establishment." Most applicants in this space would have measured referrals.
- Task 2.2's instrumentation is genuinely thorough, and the requirement that "a product failure is distinguishable from a funding restriction, provider refusal, workforce shortage, family choice, or loss to follow-up" is the correct discipline for interpreting a low establishment rate.
- Aim 1's usability study is appropriately specified: 25 families, 25 providers, 25 student workers, moderated think-aloud on standardized non-live cases, SUS and 12-item TIAS with thresholds, semi-structured interviews with a shared codebook, independent coding, discrepancy resolution, audit trail, and a joint display translating findings into engineering changes. Using non-live cases before permitting real-world action is the right sequencing.
- Task 3.1's preregistration of package, price range, assignment strategy, primary commercial outcome, follow-up horizon, and decision rules *before* the first paid market opens is unusually rigorous for a commercialization aim and directly prevents post-hoc redefinition of success.
- Task 3.3's cost accounting is honest in ways applicants usually avoid: acquisition cost explicitly includes spend on accounts that do not convert, and cost to serve explicitly includes navigator time and exception handling — the two lines that most often hide a negative margin.
- Willingness to pay is correctly demoted to "a pricing hypothesis, not commercial proof," and is elicited only after sufficient use.
- Failure handling is credible and specific: prespecified alternatives proceed in sequence (correct value gaps, reduce cost, repackage or reprice, retest), and the application commits to preserving and publishing evidence and stopping rather than scaling a failing model.

**Weaknesses**
- **The primary Aim 2 success criterion does not exist in the application.** Table 2 states only that the day-90 proportion will be "estimated with prespecified precision," and the GO/NO-GO text conditions paid expansion on establishment "meet[ing] the prespecified success criterion" — a criterion that is never specified. Two of seven Aim 1 criteria are similarly empty ("prespecified completeness across representative pathways"). The application specifies thresholds precisely everywhere else (SUS ≥72, TIAS ≥5, ≥90%, ≥50%, ≥4.0/5), which makes the omission at the single most consequential gate read as unresolved rather than merely unwritten. Reviewers cannot score a gate whose threshold will be chosen after review.
- **N≈400 is not justified.** It is described as a "target... subject to final power and recruitment justification before activation," with the final N "fixed by power/feasibility analysis." No power calculation, no target precision, no anticipated establishment rate, and no attrition allowance appear anywhere. At N=400 and p≈0.5 the 95% CI half-width is roughly ±5 points; whether that is adequate cannot be assessed because the decision it informs is unstated.
- **The primary endpoint cannot detect whether the innovation did the work.** A family confirming establishment at day 90 is fully consistent with human navigators — Olera already employs two full-time call-center staff, and the Research Strategy repeatedly routes unsupported and ambiguous workflows to a human — executing every consequential step. Task 2.2 captures human interventions in the data, but no endpoint, criterion, or prespecified target in Table 2 measures the share of tasks executed autonomously, the rate at which families grant agent permissions, or the establishment rate by execution mode. Aim 2 could be fully successful and say nothing about Innovation 2.
- **Aim 2 is single-arm and therefore cannot support the institutional value proposition.** No concurrent comparator, no usual-care arm, no waitlist, no stepped-wedge. Yet the Commercialization Plan's entire institutional engine rests on the claim that establishing care reduces downstream utilization. A staged multi-market rollout is already planned; a stepped-wedge or sequential-activation comparison was available at essentially no incremental cost and was not proposed. Reviewers will ask why.
- **≥90% expected-action agreement is a weak bar for consequential autonomous action, and it is not adjudicated independently.** One action in ten diverging from expectation, before deployment against benefit applications for vulnerable elders, warrants stratification by severity — a trivial deviation and an incorrect submission are not the same event — and the criterion contains no such stratification. The Progress Report separately claims Aim 1 "verifies closed-loop execution against blinded expert review" and the Commercialization Plan says Aim 1 "independently verifies"; the Approach text describes only comparison against an internally constructed "technical test set" with no blinding or external adjudication anywhere.
- **The field-learning criterion measures provenance, not correctness.** "Field observations with provenance and QA — 100% of persisted operational updates" can be fully satisfied by a system that learns and propagates false facts with impeccable metadata. No accuracy target, no independent verification sample, no staleness or decay policy, and no re-verification interval are specified for a knowledge layer whose whole premise is that local operational truth changes rapidly.
- **The ≥50% screened-to-placement criterion is contradicted by the applicant's own data.** The Commercialization Plan reports ~900 applications, 100 accepted, 25 placed. If "screened" corresponds to "accepted," the observed rate is 25% and the criterion is double it, with no justification for the improvement.
- **Family recruitment feasibility is asserted, not evidenced.** No funnel is given at any stage: visitors → account → completed assessment → eligible index episode → day-90 confirmation. National organic traffic is ~15,500/month spread across "nearly every county"; concentrating ~400 verified index episodes into a small number of counties within roughly a year is a different problem from national traffic, and paid acquisition is invoked without a single CAC figure.
- **Aim 3's retention measurement is not achievable within the award.** Paid conversion begins in Year 3; retention is to be "reported at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months as available"; Task 3.3 asserts these measurements "will directly replace the... retention... assumptions used in the Commercialization Plan." Twelve-month retention will exist for approximately no customers.
- **The market count is undetermined in the Approach itself.** Task 2.1 defers the number and boundaries of markets; Task 3.2 defers the number of paid markets to "the final experimental and revenue architecture in Aim 2." Since sample size, operating cost, provider density, worker supply, and revenue are all functions of market count, an unfixed market count leaves the reviewer unable to assess feasibility of anything downstream.
- Aim 3, Task 3.4 does not produce institutional-buyer evidence; it produces the *specification* for a future study ("the population, endpoints, data linkage, comparison design, and follow-up required for a subsequent payer... proof-of-concept"), plus modeled avoided cost explicitly labeled non-causal. This is intellectually honest and commercially insufficient for what the Commercialization Plan monetizes in Year 4.
- Table 3's "Independent financial validation — Delivered where commitment remains applicable" hedges a commitment the Commercialization Plan asserts flatly as "external CPA validation of the commercial unit-economics model."

### 5. Environment — Score: 4

**Strengths**
- The operating environment is real and in production, not proposed: a nationally deployed platform, an expert-curated database of 72,000+ provider and aid-program records across all fifty states with 578 program guides, 700+ providers who have claimed listings, and organic traffic grown 10× since 2023 at near-zero acquisition cost.
- Four peer-reviewed evaluations with family caregivers, two of them cited with full journal references (JMIR Aging 2024; JMIR Form Res 2026), establish that this company completes and publishes its research obligations.
- Human-subjects capacity is external and appropriate: Clemson University leads the academic effort, Fan holds every protocol, and Ory advises as she did on Phase IIB. The company is not self-reviewing its own research.
- No regulatory dependency: the application states clearly that no FDA approval is required and no in-licensing is needed, removing two common sources of environment risk.
- Compliance infrastructure is described concretely rather than gestured at: ADC accounting/CPA support, specialized legal and compliance counsel, HIPAA-aligned data practices, referral-compliance review, and federal research/IRB/data-stewardship experience.
- The SBIR history section answers every required question directly and without evasion, including the unflattering answers.

**Weaknesses**
- The commercialization track record is, by the applicant's own disclosure, effectively nil: SBIR/STTR-derived revenue of 100%, 100%, 100%, ~100%, and ~100% across FY2021–FY2025, with FY2025 one-off pilot fees under 1%, against ~$5.7M in NIA support. The NIH Environment criterion asks directly how successful the company has been at commercializing prior SBIR work, and the honest answer here is "not yet at all." The applicant's explanation — that monetization was deliberately held until it could occur without charging families or gating referrals, and that provider willingness to pay requires geographic concentration research funding was not scoped to create — is coherent and partially persuasive, but it remains an explanation for the absence of a track record rather than a track record.
- The team is very small for the proposed scope: founders, one full-time engineer of roughly two years, two full-time call-center personnel, part-time marketing, and two part-time research assistants. Year 1 alone requires building a seven-domain longitudinal state model, bounded agent execution with permission gates and scheduling, browser automation, document assembly, email/SMS/fax integration, a field-learning knowledge layer with QA, and staffing infrastructure through employer-confirmed placement — while running a 75-participant IRB study. Neither document contains an engineering hiring plan or effort allocation.
- The company has apparently never raised private capital in six years of operation. Neither document discloses any equity financing, and 100% federal revenue implies none. This bears directly on the Environment question of whether the SBC can sustain and grow itself, and on the Fundraising Plan's credibility.
- Prior NIA support is stated as running 2021–2027, overlapping the proposed three-year CRP. Neither document addresses concurrent-effort allocation across the overlapping period.

### CRP Progress Report

**Strengths**
- The report is organized around risk retirement rather than activity, and the logic is genuinely cumulative: each stage tested one uncertainty before the dependent layer was built. Reviewers found this the most persuasive part of the package.
- Every claim is tied to a specific funding source, which correctly separates what NIH paid for from what the company tested on its own capital — an unusually clean answer to the NOFO's request for progress "beyond the scope of the Phase II project."
- The evidence is concrete and mostly verifiable: 72,000+ records and 578 guides; usability 4.57/5, acceptance 5.83/7 at four weeks (n=65), multi-agent 5.73/7 (n=31); ~50→500+ daily organic visits; 300+ provider discovery conversations and 700+ claimed listings; ~900 workforce applicants with 20+ placements; four providers trialed staffing and three paid.
- The statement that manual delivery was deliberate — "to learn the workflow before building the infrastructure that automates it" — is exactly the right answer to the reviewer question of why a company would hand-deliver a product it proposes to automate.
- Third-party investment readiness is described without inflation, and the disclaimer "these relationships are not financing commitments" is stated twice. Reviewers credited the restraint.
- The statement of what remains unretired is accurate and does not overclaim.

**Weaknesses**
- The report contains the market-count statement — "Aim 2 measures established care and stakeholder value in **two real markets**" — that directly contradicts the Commercialization Plan's eight and Aim 2's own deferral. Of the three market counts in the package, this is the one embedded in the section reviewers read as the applicant's own summary of its plan.
- It asserts that "Aim 1 verifies closed-loop execution against **blinded expert review**," a verification method that appears nowhere in Aim 1. This is the kind of unsupported strengthening that costs credibility precisely because the rest of the section is so disciplined.
- The willingness-to-pay evidence is thinner than the surrounding prose implies: three paying providers, at $50–$275 per placement plus $275/month, is a demonstration that the number is not zero. The Commercialization Plan then bases a $250 base case on it.
- "Placed more than 20" here versus "placed 25" in the Commercialization Plan is a small inconsistency in the package's single most load-bearing operating statistic.
- The claim that the Phase IIB review "assessed its commercial potential as extremely high" quotes a prior summary statement. It is accurate but reviewers generally discount an applicant's citation of its own previous review, and it occupies space that a cost-per-placement or conversion figure would use better.
- The cross-reference "the commercial endpoints in Tables 4 and 5 are the ones those investors said they would need to see" is broken: the Research Strategy has no Tables 4 or 5, and the Commercialization Plan's Tables 4 and 5 are the SBIR history and the market-acceptance hurdles, neither of which lists commercial endpoints.

### Commercialization Plan

**Strengths**
- The Statement of Need is a correct and well-argued description of a real financing gap: investors must underwrite completion before institutional value is established, while institutional buyers require evidence before they will purchase. The applicant states specifically what would not otherwise happen, and why.
- The rejection of the two obvious monetization shortcuts is principled and commercially reasoned, not merely ethical: charging families raises the barrier for households already unable to afford care, and charging providers for referrals introduces steering incentives and excludes some federally reimbursed providers.
- The revenue model is arithmetically self-consistent throughout. 8 markets × 10 hires/month × $250 = $240K annualized; six paid-month equivalents = ~$120K in Year 3; 15 markets = $450K in Year 4; 25 markets = $750K staffing plus $750K institutional = ~$1.5M in Year 5. Every figure checks.
- Table 7 is a model of disclosure discipline: each input is labeled as a published benchmark, an Olera assumption, or a CRP hypothesis, and the $250K institutional figure is explicitly flagged "Planning hypothesis, not a benchmark."
- The institutional engine is modeled conservatively and correctly: zero institutional revenue during the CRP, and evidence-gated contracts rather than an unvalidated PMPM multiplied across a hypothetical health plan. The application says so in as many words, and reviewers noticed.
- Table 8's staged commercial framing (Build → Validate free → Monetize → Expand → Scale) matches the Research Strategy's gating logic, and Table 9's management timeline attaches a financing decision to each stage.
- The county as the unit of commercialization is a genuinely useful construct: it makes acquisition cost, throughput, and care establishment measurable in the same denominator, and it supports a replication rather than market-share growth story across 3,100+ units.
- Section 5's "temporal barriers to replication" argument is the right argument for this asset class — distribution, national data infrastructure, workforce relationships, and accumulated longitudinal records that a new entrant cannot obtain retrospectively.

**Weaknesses**
- The single input that drives all staffing revenue — 10 successful hires per county per month, described as "conservative" — has no supporting evidence and is contradicted in magnitude by the applicant's own pilot. The pilot placed 25 workers total. The Year 3 base case requires 480 successful hires in six paid months across eight counties, roughly 20× lifetime performance; the Year 3 exit run rate implies 960/year. At the pilot's ~2.8% applicant-to-placement yield, 480 hires requires on the order of 17,000 applicants in six months, or ~2,100 per county, against a pilot that produced ~900 in one market over multiple semesters.
- The long-range illustration is arithmetically incompatible with the stated workforce wedge. Even at the *target* 50% screened-to-placement rate, 60,000 hires at 500 counties requires ~120,000 screened candidates — approximately 92% of the combined annual national MD (54,699) and NursingCAS (75,078) applicant pools the plan itself cites. At the observed pilot yield it requires ~2.1M applicants, roughly 16× those pools. The hedge that "these two pipelines are not the full addressable workforce" is then carrying the entire scale case.
- There is no cost side. No cost to serve, no contribution margin, no gross margin, not even an illustrative range, anywhere in the plan. A "conservative base case" that models only revenue is not a base case. This matters acutely at $250 per hire with a ~36-applicants-per-placement funnel: the plan's viability turns on a cost figure it does not contain.
- No provider-conversion funnel is given. 700 providers have claimed listings and 3 have ever paid, but the plan never states how many paying accounts each market requires, what conversion rate is assumed from claimed to paid, or what repeat-purchase rate underlies "10 hires per month" from a given account.
- The free-to-paid transition is unaddressed as a commercial risk. Staffing is deployed free to providers throughout Year 2 and priced in Year 3 to what appear to be the same markets; nothing addresses anchoring, or the possibility that Year 2 volume is a function of the zero price.
- The institutional revenue line is not supported by the evidence the CRP produces. Year 4 books ~$250K from ~1 relationship, priced against a single-arm establishment proportion, explicitly non-causal modeled avoided cost, and a study design for a proof-of-concept that has not been run. MA plan and ACO procurement cycles alone make a Year 4 contract of that size aggressive.
- The IP position is weak against the criterion as written. Trade secret, copyright, and trademark, with patents only to be "evaluated," is a thin portfolio for a company whose defensibility argument is that its integrated asset is hard to recreate. No filings, applications, or provisional coverage are disclosed.
- Section 7 states the CRP "applies this process across approximately eight markets," preempting a decision the Research Strategy explicitly defers, and conflicting with the Progress Report's two.

### Human Subjects Design Visible in the Research Strategy

> **Note:** Full Human Subjects and Clinical Trials materials were not supplied and are assumed adequate for this mock review, per instruction. No determination is issued on their basis. The findings below concern only design features intrinsic to the Research Strategy that no separate Human Subjects section could cure. **Assessment: CONCERN.**

**Strengths**
- Human protection is architectural rather than procedural. Aim 2 opens only after prespecified technical criteria are met, required approvals cannot be bypassed, users demonstrate acceptable usability and control, and no critical human-protection issue remains unresolved.
- "Human protection is overriding" is stated as a standing rule, and any unresolved critical privacy, permission, unauthorized-action, or safety failure is a NO-GO for real-world execution.
- The scope limitation is genuinely protective and clearly drawn: the system does not diagnose, prescribe, place clinical orders, or make clinical decisions, and authorized health information may be used only to extract and execute administrative tasks already defined by the family, provider, or clinician.
- Consequential actions requiring attestation, legal authority, submission, scheduling, or family choice require human approval; unsupported or ambiguous workflows remain human-assisted; the family remains the decision-maker.
- Non-live standardized cases are used for the Aim 1 usability study, so no participant is exposed to agent action before verification.
- A pause-and-review mechanism is prespecified for critical privacy events, unauthorized consequential actions, systematic erroneous execution, and serious safety signals, with explicit provision for required IRB action and re-verification.
- Human-protection boundaries are stated to be unchanged by the introduction of payment in Aim 3.

**Weaknesses**
- The ≥90% action-agreement gate is not stratified by severity. A 10% rate of trivial deviations and a 10% rate of erroneous submissions to a state LTSS program are entirely different risk profiles, and only the latter can harm a participant. Severity stratification belongs in the Approach, not in a Human Subjects form.
- "Predefined serious safety signal" is invoked without any adverse-event taxonomy. For an intervention whose harms are administrative rather than clinical — a denied or delayed benefit, a lost eligibility window, an erroneous disclosure to a provider or agency — the harm categories are non-obvious and need to be defined where the study is designed.
- No independent safety monitoring body is named. This study lets software take consequential real-world actions affecting benefits access for a vulnerable population; independent monitoring is warranted on design grounds regardless of whether the work meets the clinical-trial definition.
- Decisional capacity and surrogate authorization are not addressed anywhere in the Research Strategy, despite the fact that the mechanism *is* authorization: a family "authorizes" the system to prepare an LTSS application or contact agencies. Who may grant that authorization, how capacity is assessed, and what happens when the care recipient and the authorizing family member disagree are design decisions intrinsic to the intervention. *The procedural elements of consent are assumed adequately addressed in the full Human Subjects materials; the authorization model itself is not.*
- "Permissioned browser automation" against government and provider portals is proposed without addressing portal terms of use or the risk that automated access jeopardizes a participant's own account or application status. This is a participant-risk question, not only a legal one.
- Aim 2 sets no ceiling on human-protection incidents below the "critical" threshold. A workflow producing a steady rate of non-critical permission or execution errors has no defined stopping rule.

### E. Top Score-Driving Strengths

1. A first-rate Significance section: urgent, well-cited unmet need with a clean and consistently maintained separation of user, participant, and buyer, and explicit acknowledgment that the larger market is not yet purchasable.
2. A real technical thesis — making the care-establishment pathway computable — that is argued rather than asserted, and that correctly identifies representation as the prerequisite for both automation and measurement.
3. A gated architecture in which technical verification precedes human exposure, real-world value precedes price, and human protection overrides everything, with an explicit commitment to publish a negative result and stop.
4. A cumulative, source-attributed, honestly-bounded risk-retirement history: national deployment, four peer-reviewed evaluations, 700+ claimed provider listings, ~900 workforce applicants, 25 placements, and three providers who paid real money.
5. An endpoint that is genuinely hard and genuinely right — family-confirmed establishment of the documented need within 90 days, with referrals, approvals, matches, and job offers explicitly excluded.
6. Unusual disclosure discipline in the revenue model: hypotheses labeled as hypotheses, benchmarks labeled as benchmarks, zero institutional revenue assumed during the award, arithmetic that verifies throughout, and CAC defined to include non-converting spend.

### F. Top Score-Driving Weaknesses

1. The primary Aim 2 success criterion and the N justification are absent; a gate whose threshold is chosen after review cannot be scored.
2. No endpoint distinguishes agent-executed from human-executed pathways, so Aim 2 can succeed without validating the central innovation.
3. The staffing throughput assumption driving all revenue is unsupported and ~20× demonstrated performance, and the long-range illustration exceeds the applicant's own cited labor pools.
4. Market count differs materially between the two documents (two vs. approximately eight vs. undetermined), destabilizing sample size, cost, and revenue simultaneously.
5. Aim 2 is single-arm, so the institutional value proposition the Commercialization Plan monetizes in Years 4–5 cannot be causally supported by the evidence the Research Strategy produces — a gap the Research Strategy concedes and the Commercialization Plan does not.
6. No cost side anywhere in the Commercialization Plan: no cost to serve, no margin, no illustrative range.
7. Weak formal IP and a Fundraising Plan resting on relationships the applicant twice describes as non-commitments, from a company with no disclosed private financing history and ~100% federal revenue for five consecutive years.
8. A very lean team against a Year 1 engineering load, with no hiring plan, no effort allocation, and a circular sequence in which non-founder staff are needed to produce the milestone that is supposed to justify hiring them.

### G. Likely Discussion Dynamics

- **The market-count discrepancy will open discussion** and will be raised as a proxy for whether the applicant has actually converged on a plan. Because sample size, cost, revenue, and workforce demand all depend on it, reviewers will treat two-versus-eight as evidence that the operating plan and the financial model were written against different assumptions. The applicant's defense — that market count is properly derived from a power analysis — is sound but is undermined by the Commercialization Plan having already fixed it at eight and built a revenue model on it.
- **The strongest advocate's argument** will be that this is the correct use of the CRP mechanism: a real, deployed, published, used product with no commercial proof, a company that has been honest about exactly which risks remain, and a gated plan that will produce a clean answer either way. That advocate will emphasize that the applicant proposes to measure whether care is *established*, not referred — a standard almost no competitor holds itself to.
- **The skeptic's argument** will be arithmetic, and it will be delivered in two moves: 25 lifetime placements versus 480 required in six paid months; and 60,000 hires requiring ~92% of the national MD and nursing applicant pools at the applicant's own target conversion. The skeptic will add that ~$5.7M of federal support has produced under 1% non-federal revenue and that this application asks for ~$4M more.
- **The pivotal exchange** will be over whether the missing Aim 2 threshold is a drafting defect or a substantive one. Advocates will call it correctable; skeptics will note that the same document specifies SUS ≥72, TIAS ≥5, ≥90%, ≥50%, and ≥4.0/5 with precision, which makes the omission at the decisive gate look like unresolved thinking. This argument determines whether Approach is scored 4 or 5 and therefore whether the overall lands at 3 or 4.
- **A methodologically strong reviewer will raise the single-arm design** and will point out that a stepped-wedge or staggered-activation comparison was available essentially for free given that markets are already being activated sequentially. This is the criticism most likely to change minds, because it identifies a fixable design choice that would materially increase the value of everything downstream.
- **Expect a Significance split of 2 versus 3**, turning on how much of the commercialization-probability question reviewers import into Significance versus quarantine in Environment and the Commercialization Plan assessment.
- **Expect the automation-share point to be raised late** and to land hard, because once articulated it is unanswerable from the document: nothing in Aim 2 prevents a successful result produced entirely by human navigators.

## OVERALL IMPACT SCORE: 4 (Very Good)

**Why this score.** The application targets a large, urgent, well-documented problem with a technically substantive and non-obvious thesis, proposes a correctly gated program that would produce an interpretable answer in either direction, and comes from a team that has delivered against two prior NIA awards and has real if small commercial traction. It is also the right project for this mechanism: the applicant is demonstrably past feasibility and demonstrably short of commercial proof, and says so plainly. Against that, the application's decisive quantitative commitments are either missing (the Aim 2 success criterion, the power justification, cost to serve), unsupported (staffing throughput, the ≥50% placement rate, family recruitment yield), or inconsistent between the two documents (market count, verification method, the institutional evidence the plan monetizes). A 4 reflects a proposal whose architecture would likely support a sustained influence on the market offering, but whose execution parameters a panel cannot currently verify.

**Why not one point better (3).** A 3 would require the reviewer to believe the CRP will produce a decisive commercial answer. It cannot yet, for three specific reasons. The primary gate has no threshold, so "success" in Aim 2 is undefined at the time of review. The primary endpoint does not distinguish the proposed innovation from the human process it replaces, so a positive result would be ambiguous about the product. And the Commercialization Plan's larger revenue engine depends on causal evidence the Research Strategy explicitly declines to produce, so the most valuable commercial question survives the award unretired. Any one of these might be absorbed; together they mean the application's own logic does not guarantee the answer it promises.

**Why not one point worse (5).** A 5 would imply the strengths are ordinary. They are not. The customer architecture is clearer than most applications in this space achieve; the endpoint standard — family-confirmed establishment, with referrals and approvals explicitly excluded — is harder than the field's convention and is the correct standard; the risk history is cumulative, source-attributed, and honest about what remains; the cost accounting deliberately includes the two lines applicants usually hide; the pricing offer is preregistered before conversion is observed; and the applicant commits in writing to publishing a negative result and stopping rather than scaling a failing model. The revenue model, whatever the merits of its central input, is arithmetically consistent and explicitly labels its hypotheses as hypotheses. The identified weaknesses are specific and largely correctable within the existing design rather than reflecting a misconceived project.
