## RESUME AND SUMMARY OF DISCUSSION

This SBIR Commercialization Readiness Pilot application proposes to complete and commercialize the Olera CareNavigator, an eldercare navigation platform developed across NIA Phase I/II Fast-Track and Phase IIB awards, by adding three integrated capabilities: a longitudinal Care Establishment Model that renders the eldercare pathway as computable case state; a suite of bounded AI agents that execute the administrative work between a care plan and a confirmed care start and that generate operational field knowledge as a byproduct of execution; and a data-directed Caregiver Staffing product that recruits pre-health students into the direct-care workforce where labor scarcity is preventing care from starting. Three sequential aims engineer and technically verify the system (Aim 1), validate it in eight free county markets over nine months (Aim 2), and test paid provider economics and market-entry replication in eight new markets (Aim 3).

Enthusiasm for the problem and for the applicant's continuity was uniformly high. Reviewers agreed that unmet need among older adults is a major public-health problem, that the documents quantify it well, and that the applicant's choice of *established care* — rather than referral, eligibility, or information delivery — as the terminal outcome is a genuinely superior framing that most competitors do not adopt. The company's national deployment, low-cost organic distribution, curated national data infrastructure, four peer-reviewed evaluations, and an unusually honest and arithmetically coherent five-year financial model were all viewed favorably. Reviewers also credited the applicant for explicitly labeling its pricing and institutional-revenue figures as hypotheses rather than forecasts, a discipline that is uncommon in this mechanism.

Concerns concentrated in two areas, and they are related. First, the **Approach cannot attribute the outcome it measures.** Aim 2's primary endpoint — the proportion of family episodes reaching verified care or aid establishment — is single-arm, has no comparator, no external benchmark, and no baseline rate from the applicant's own five years of national operation. The endpoint is defined by the system ("primary need... assigned during the assessment stage"), adjudicated primarily by family self-report ("when possible" corroborated), and gated at a threshold (95% CI lower bound > 35%) whose selection is not justified against any status-quo rate. Reviewers noted that this design can produce a number but cannot establish that the number was produced by the CRP-funded innovations, which is precisely the inference the Commercialization Plan requires it to support.

Second, **the funded technology and the tested revenue engine are not the same product.** The great majority of the Aim 1 engineering risk and cost — the Care Establishment Model, the agent suite, browser automation, the field-learning loop — supports CareNavigator, which the applicant commits to keeping free to families and free to providers. The only product monetized within the award is Caregiver Staffing, a recruiting funnel that does not require any of that infrastructure and whose fully-penetrated base-case market, using the applicant's own arithmetic, is approximately $7.4M in annual revenue. The institutional CareNavigator engine that carries essentially all of the long-run revenue is deferred entirely to the post-CRP period and is gated on evidence the CRP's design will not produce.

A minority view held that these are acceptable staging decisions for a company at this maturity, that revealed provider willingness to pay (three of four pilot providers paid ~$275 per placement) is more than most CRP applicants can show, and that the go/no-go architecture is above average. The majority position was that the strengths are real but that the central evidence-generating design is not rigorous enough to unlock the institutional market the financing thesis depends on, and that the Fundraising Plan does not yet demonstrate the ability to secure third-party capital equal to or exceeding the requested federal funds. On balance, strengths do not outweigh weaknesses sufficiently to place this application in the top tier for this mechanism.

# CRITIQUE 1 — Integrated Primary Reviewer

## Overall Impact

This is a well-constructed application from a credible, continuous team addressing a problem of high and rising public-health importance, and the applicant has done more real-world de-risking than most companies arriving at CRP. The framing of *established care* as the north-star outcome is genuinely differentiated, the instrumented-pathway concept is a defensible long-run asset, and the three-aim gate structure reflects sound thinking about sequencing exposure. The financial model is arithmetically coherent and refreshingly explicit about which numbers are benchmarks and which are hypotheses.

The application does not reach the top tier for two structural reasons. The Approach generates a descriptive, uncontrolled, self-adjudicated care-establishment rate and then asks the Commercialization Plan to convert that rate, plus external literature and economic modeling, into a "contracting-ready institutional evidence package." Sophisticated risk-bearing buyers do not contract on single-arm self-report, and the application does not explain why they would. Simultaneously, the only revenue engine actually tested within the award has a full-penetration base case of roughly $7.4M per year at the applicant's own price and throughput assumptions, and the post-CRP financing plan (a $3–5M raise against $1.4M and $1.85M Year 4–5 operating requirements with $90K and $400K in revenue) does not reach self-sustainability inside its own five-year window. The result is a project likely to produce a well-instrumented, operationally informative pilot and a modestly profitable staffing line, rather than one likely to exert a sustained, powerful influence on the market offering within a credible commercialization horizon.

**Why not one point better (3):** A 3 would require that the CRP's evidence actually retire the assumption the financing thesis rests on. It does not. There is no comparator, no baseline, no independent adjudication of the primary endpoint, no pre-specified justification for the 35% gate, and no attribution of care establishment to agent execution — despite the platform being uniquely well-positioned to randomize the agent-execution offer at near-zero cost. Aim 3 tests neither the institutional buyer nor price elasticity, so the two variables that dominate the revenue model are the two the CRP will not measure.

**Why not one point worse (5):** A 5 would require that the commercial premise be speculative. It is not. Providers have paid real money at a price consistent with the model; the student workforce channel produced 900+ applicants and 25 placements; organic distribution is real and cheap; the endpoint definition is better than the field's; the go/no-go criteria, though internally sourced, are pre-specified and include explicit no-go language; and the applicant repeatedly declines to overclaim. This is a serious, competent application with a fixable evidence-design problem, not a weak one.

## 1. Significance — Score: 3

### Strengths

- The unmet need is established with unusual specificity and good sourcing: $80,080 annual cost of full-time home care, ~$58B in unclaimed aid, 63.3% of home-care providers declining cases for staffing reasons in 2023, 9.7M projected direct-care vacancies 2024–2034, and the demographic scissors of an 82M over-65 population against family caregivers per adult over 80 falling from seven to four. The linkage from unmet ADL need to ED use, readmission, nursing-home placement, and mortality is asserted with citations rather than rhetoric.
- The choice of *care established* as the terminal outcome is the single strongest conceptual contribution in either document. The application correctly identifies that the field's standard endpoints — information delivered, referral made, eligibility determined — systematically overstate success, and it builds its entire architecture around closing that loop. This is a real insight and it differentiates the product.
- Market segmentation is clearly and honestly tabulated (Table 1, Research Strategy). Four segments are named with their commercial relationship and the reason each relationship is structured as it is. The decision to keep families and referral-side providers free is defended on substantive grounds (payment barriers for households already unable to afford care; steering incentives and federal-reimbursement constraints on paid referral) rather than as a growth tactic.
- Caregiver Staffing is a well-chosen beachhead on the correct logic: it addresses a problem providers already pay to solve, it is the half of the problem families cannot solve themselves, and capacity is a hard precondition for care establishment. The value ratio is favorable and stated — roughly $250 per hire against a published ~$2,700 replacement cost and 75% median annual turnover.
- The competitive landscape is presented as a capability matrix across the pathway (Table 2) rather than a list of company names, which is the right analytic frame and makes the whitespace claim inspectable.
- Adoption hurdles are stated candidly and specifically for each of the four constituencies, including the acknowledgment that risk-bearing institutions face the highest evidence threshold.

### Weaknesses

- **Commercial potential — the criterion's explicit question — is only moderately supported.** Using the applicant's own base case (2 successful hires per active market per month at $250 per hire = $6,000 per market-year) and the applicant's own total addressable market (1,233 counties), the fully-penetrated near-term revenue engine is approximately $7.4M in annual revenue. The applicant's own scenario table caps at $3.0M annually at 500 markets. For a mechanism intended to bridge to substantial third-party financing, a beachhead of this magnitude is a material limitation that neither document acknowledges or addresses.
- **The market count contains a material arithmetic error.** Section 8 asserts "a total addressable market of 1233 mid-sized counties, representing ~66% of the 3000+ counties in the US." 1,233 of 3,000+ is approximately 39–41%, not 66%. Section 4 separately states there are "more than 3,100 such geographic units nationally" and implies all are addressable. A reviewer checking the one number that sizes the market will find it wrong and will discount adjacent quantitative claims.
- **The urgency of the paying customer's need is conflated with the urgency of the beneficiary's need.** The documents establish overwhelming urgency for families. Provider urgency is established for staffing generally, but not for *Olera's* staffing product specifically over Indeed, existing agencies, or the named competitor CareYaya. Institutional-buyer urgency is asserted from the GUIDE Model precedent and an association between unmet HCBS need and acute utilization (52% vs. 34% ED use), but no institutional buyer is shown to be actively seeking this category, and no discovery with MA plans, MCOs, or ACOs is reported anywhere in either document.
- **The customer-needs evidence is asymmetric.** 200+ customer-discovery conversations and four peer-reviewed studies support the family and provider segments. Zero discovery is reported for risk-bearing institutions — the segment that carries approximately 62% of the Year 5 base-case revenue and essentially all long-term revenue.
- The competitive matrix awards Olera a full-capability mark in every column, but that row is labeled "after the CRP" and is therefore the hypothesis, not evidence. More importantly, general-purpose AI is scored as having no execution capability. Given that the application's own Innovation 2 is essentially form-filling, portal navigation, and outbound communication — capabilities frontier labs are actively commoditizing on a timescale shorter than this award — the failure to address that erosion risk is the most conspicuous omission in the competitive analysis.
- The distribution claim of near-zero acquisition cost is undercut by the applicant's own plan: family recruitment in Aims 2 and 3 relies on paid, geo-targeted advertising, because organic national traffic (~15,500 monthly visitors spread across the country) does not concentrate in eight specific counties. The documents never reconcile the "near-zero CAC" positioning with the geographically concentrated acquisition the CRP actually requires.

## 2. Investigator(s) — Score: 3

*Assumption stated explicitly: biosketches, letters of support, and consultant commitment letters were not supplied and are assumed adequate. No score reduction is applied for their absence. The assessment below rests only on team information contained in the two supplied documents.*

### Strengths

- Exceptional continuity. Falohun was PI on both prior NIA awards and leads product, technical integration, and the Aim 1 engineering workstream. DuBose was Co-Investigator on Phase I–IIB and leads research integration and cross-aim operations. The proposed work is led by substantially the same team that built and evaluated the system, which materially reduces execution risk on the engineering and research workstreams.
- Complementary and appropriate expertise across the disciplines the project requires: applied AI/software engineering (Falohun), clinical and health-services perspective (DuBose, MD/MBA), independent human-subjects and mixed-methods execution (Fan, Clemson), and senior aging-science guidance (Ory, >6 years advising, 20 years at NIA).
- Governance is explicitly allocated rather than left implicit. Falohun retains final go/no-go authority and owns agent-execution QA and provider commercialization; DuBose owns the milestone calendar and safety-event coordination; Fan/Clemson owns human-subjects protocol execution. Independent validation is assigned by year and type (technical/statistical, statistical, CPA/economic) in Table 4 of the Commercialization Plan.
- The use of external expertise is proportionate and long-standing rather than assembled for the application — a distinction the documents make explicitly and credibly.
- Designating an existing operations staff member as Market Operations Lead specifically to execute the non-founder market-entry test in Aim 3 is a thoughtful design choice: it converts a team-capability question into a measured experiment rather than an assertion.

### Weaknesses

- **No demonstrated commercialization track record.** Across approximately $5.7M of federal R&D and five years, total realized commercial revenue is described as "multiple one-off paid pilots," with the only quantified instance being three providers paying approximately $275 per placement across 25 placements — on the order of $7,000. SBIR/STTR funding is reported as 100% of company revenue in each of FY2021–FY2023 and approximately 100% in FY2024–FY2025. Under a criterion that asks directly "how successful have the PD(s)/PI(s) been in commercializing technologies and discoveries in the past?", the honest answer visible in these documents is: not yet. The Commercialization Plan's framing of this as "limited and by design" is a reasonable explanation but does not substitute for a track record.
- **No dedicated commercial leadership, and the application says so.** The Commercialization Plan states that "Aim 3 itself is intentionally the experiment that determines whether the company can execute commercialization beyond founder intuition," and that dedicated commercial, customer-success, data/compliance, and finance capabilities will be internalized only "when their workload and strategic importance justify full-time leadership." Reviewers will read this as candid, and also as confirmation that the CRP's central commercialization risk is being carried by a CEO who has not yet done it and a quarterly advisor.
- **Team capacity appears materially under-scaled for the proposed scope.** The described staff for the entire program is: two founders; one existing full-time software engineer plus one added at CRP start; two full-time family/provider operations personnel; a part-time marketer; two part-time research assistants; and the Clemson team. Against that, Year 1 requires building a seven-domain longitudinal state model, a multi-tool agent suite (APIs, permissioned browser automation, document assembly, email, SMS, fax, telephone, scheduling), an autonomous field-learning loop with human QA, and staffing infrastructure — while concurrently running a 75-participant moderated IRB study. Years 2 and 3 require operating sixteen county markets, tracking approximately 880 longitudinal family episodes to confirmed terminal states with outbound confirmation, placing 150 workers, running provider sales, and producing an institutional evidence package. Two engineers and two operations staff for this is the single most common reason CRP projects miss milestones, and the documents contain no contingency for it beyond "scope narrows before the calendar compresses."
- **The commercialization advisor's role is advisory, not committed.** The Commercialization Plan states that Qu "will advise Olera quarterly" and that "he mayl [sic] support introductions." Named funds (Ziegler, Equitage Ventures, 7Wire Ventures, Alumni Ventures) are described as being "in his network." Per this review's standing assumption, his letter of support is assumed to substantiate the advisory role as described — but the plan's credibility depends on more than that role as described, and the documents do not close the gap between advisory relationship and financing capability.
- The statement that "Our investor advisors agree that longitudinal outcomes... would materially improve Olera's investability" attributes a favorable conclusion to unnamed parties without characterizing who they are or what they have actually indicated. This conflates advice with interest and interest with commitment.

## 3. Innovation — Score: 4

### Strengths

- **The Care Establishment Model is a real and load-bearing engineering contribution.** The observation that reliable automation of a fragmented pathway first requires a computational representation of that pathway — case state with substates, timestamped events, geography, provenance, evidence, ownership of the next action, and explicit terminal states — is correct and is the kind of unglamorous abstraction that determines whether an agentic system works. The application is right that no directly reusable model of the eldercare operational ecosystem exists.
- **The field-learning closed loop is the most defensible innovation in the application.** The insight that operational truth in eldercare is local, rapidly changing, and largely absent from the public web — and that execution agents can therefore function as structured field researchers whose findings are normalized with source, geography, time, and provenance and gated behind human QA before entering the production knowledge layer — is genuinely differentiated. The illustration is well-chosen: a provider that publicly accepts a payer but reports by phone that weekend capacity is unavailable until November. This asset compounds through use, cannot be reconstructed retrospectively, and is the most plausible durable advantage described anywhere in either document.
- **The permission architecture is thoughtfully specified.** Actions requiring attestation, legal authority, identity verification, or clinical judgment are bounded to remain human-controlled; agents are explicitly prohibited from enacting diagnosis, treatment, or clinical interpretation; permissions distinguish the platform user from the person whose information or benefits are involved; and agents are barred from inferring legal decision-making authority from caregiver status. This is more careful than most agentic health applications and reflects genuine domain understanding.
- **The workforce wedge is a legitimately non-obvious insight.** Existing channels compete for workers already circulating in a constrained labor market. Pre-health students are structurally distributed through universities, replenish annually, actively seek documented patient-facing hours, and are available on the evenings and weekends providers report as hardest to fill. This is additive supply rather than redistribution, and the 900-applicant pilot demonstrates reachability.

### Weaknesses

- **The application does not defend Innovation 2 against commoditization.** Bounded task execution — portal navigation, form assembly, outbound email/SMS/fax/phone, scheduling, persistence across delays — is the exact capability frontier AI labs are shipping and improving on a cadence faster than a three-year award. The Research Strategy's own competitive matrix concedes that general-purpose AI already has core capability on Assess, Find, and Fund. Neither document explains what remains proprietary once general agent execution is a commodity. The honest answer is probably "the state model, the provenance-gated field knowledge, and the distribution" — but the application never says so, and instead rests its innovation claim partly on the layer most likely to erode.
- **"Data-directed" workforce targeting is asserted but never tested.** Innovation 3's distinguishing claim is that Olera uses instrumented pathway data — funded family demand, provider denials and vacancies, service type, geography, shift requirements — to target new supply at actual capacity deficits. No aim compares data-directed targeting against undirected recruiting. Aim 2 deploys Staffing in the same eight markets as CareNavigator, so the two are confounded by design, and Aim 3 repeats the pattern. At the end of the award, the applicant will not be able to say whether the care-establishment instrumentation improved staffing outcomes at all. The most distinctive innovation claim is therefore the one the Approach is least equipped to evaluate.
- The Care Establishment Model, while useful, is careful applied engineering rather than a paradigm shift. Longitudinal case-state models with provenance and terminal states are standard practice in adjacent domains (closed-loop referral platforms, care-coordination systems, workflow engines). The novelty is domain-specific instantiation and completeness of coverage, not the concept.
- The application acknowledges an existing student-caregiver competitor (CareYaya) but does not analyze it. Given that the workforce wedge is the near-term revenue engine and a named competitor occupies the same wedge, a one-line mention is insufficient.
- The claim that advantages compound into a moat rests on the accumulation of executed cases, but the CRP will generate on the order of 880 episodes across sixteen counties. Whether that volume is anywhere near the threshold at which the data asset becomes commercially meaningful is never estimated.

## 4. Approach — Score: 5

*Assumption stated explicitly: full Human Subjects materials were not supplied and are assumed adequate with respect to consent, privacy, confidentiality, inclusion documentation, risk minimization, monitoring, and IRB requirements. Only issues intrinsic to the research design itself are scored below.*

### Strengths

- The three-aim architecture is logically sequenced and each aim is a genuine precondition for the next: build and technically verify before human exposure; validate free before charging; replicate in untouched markets before claiming a repeatable playbook. Excluding Aim 2 markets from the Aim 3 paid-conversion analysis because prior free exposure could bias purchasing behavior is a correct and non-obvious design decision that many applicants get wrong.
- Tasks are concrete and inspectable. Task 1.2 names the tool classes, the event-driven loop structure, the escalation paths, and the specific categories of action that must remain human-controlled. Task 1.3 specifies that autonomously retrieved data enters the production knowledge layer only after a human QA gate. These are implementable specifications, not aspirations.
- Go/no-go criteria are pre-specified in three tables with measures, thresholds, and named data sources, and the application includes genuine no-go language — narrowing, disabling, moving behind additional approval, retiring workflows, and, at the Aim 3 gate, "refine, pivot, or stop the unsupported commercial pathway rather than scale a potential invalidated product or revenue model." Willingness to state a stop condition is a real strength.
- The contingency principle — "scope narrows before the calendar compresses" — is the right governing rule and is applied concretely: only workflow classes that pass the Aim 1 gate enter Aim 2; Aim 3 does not begin before Aim 2 outcome ascertainment is complete; Wave 1 is completed and analyzed before Wave 2 opens.
- Aim 1's Task 1.5 appropriately places usability and trust testing on non-live, standardized cases in a non-production staging environment, using validated instruments (SUS, TIAS) across all three user classes, before any real-world exposure. This is the correct ordering.
- Secondary outcomes in Aim 2 are well-chosen and operationally useful: classification of every action as user-, agent-, or support-executed; time to establishment; task-level failure and fall-off points; and authorization-versus-denial rates for permission-gated agent actions.

### Weaknesses

- **The primary endpoint has no comparator, no benchmark, and no baseline, and the threshold is therefore uninterpretable.** Aim 2 will report the proportion of episodes reaching verified care or aid establishment and will progress if the 95% CI lower bound exceeds 35%. No status-quo rate is offered from any source — not from the literature, and, more damagingly, not from the applicant's own five years of national operation with 15,500 monthly visitors. Without a counterfactual, a 45% establishment rate is equally consistent with a large effect and with no effect at all. This is the central defect of the Approach, because every downstream commercial claim depends on this number meaning something.
- **The applicant is uniquely positioned to run the obvious comparison and does not propose it.** The platform assigns permission-gated agent actions to families, logs authorization and denial, and classifies every action by executor. Randomizing the offer of agent execution within Aim 2 — or, at minimum, comparing episodes in which families authorized agent execution against those in which they declined, with appropriate adjustment — would cost almost nothing and would directly test whether the CRP-funded innovations improve care establishment. Its absence means Aim 1 builds the agents, Aim 2 observes cases in which agents were sometimes used, and no aim asks whether the agents mattered.
- **The endpoint is defined and adjudicated by the sponsor.** The success state is "assigned during the assessment stage" by the system itself, and completion "requires family confirmation and, when possible, provider or aid program representative confirmation." In practice this is self-report with optional corroboration, on an outcome whose definition the sponsor controls, with no independent adjudication, no blinded review, and no pre-registration. Both the numerator and the denominator are under the applicant's control. Institutional buyers evaluating this evidence will identify this immediately.
- **The 400-episode sample size is not justified and has essentially no margin.** No power or precision calculation is presented, and no assumed underlying rate is stated. At n=400, a 95% CI around an observed rate of 40% has a lower bound of approximately 35.2%. The gate is therefore satisfied only if the true rate is approximately 40% or higher — an implicit and undisclosed design assumption that is never defended. Reviewers will note that the sample size appears reverse-engineered from the threshold rather than derived from a hypothesis.
- **The automation-share metric interacts perversely with the contingency rule.** The Aim 2 gate requires that "≥70% of approved agent actions eligible for automation" complete without human execution. Both "approved" and "eligible for automation" are defined by the applicant, and the Aim 1 contingency explicitly narrows the set of workflow classes entering Aim 2 to those that pass. Narrowing scope therefore improves the metric. As specified, this criterion can be satisfied by an arbitrarily small automation envelope, and the application does not commit to a minimum breadth of workflow classes or report the metric against the full set of administrative actions a case actually requires.
- **Aim 1's verification criteria are internal, subjective, and in one case unfalsifiable.** The agent-execution gate is "All expected actions per internal team assessment with no perceived errors in workflow logic, permission gating, tool constraints, or agent output," evaluated on an unspecified number of mock cases in an internal staging environment by the team that built the system. "No perceived errors" is not a measurable criterion. No test-set size, no case-mix specification, no acceptance rate, no adversarial or red-team testing, and no independent review are specified for a system that will subsequently submit government benefit applications on behalf of older adults.
- **The field-learning gate of "100% of new data acquired, normalized, and persisted" is not a credible criterion for an autonomous LLM-mediated pipeline.** Either it is trivially satisfied by defining success as "whatever persisted," or it is unachievable. A precision/recall specification against a human-adjudicated gold standard is what this task requires.
- **The safety gate turns on an undefined term and a vendor document.** Both the Aim 1 and Aim 2 gates hinge on "no unresolved critical event breaking best practice guidelines for safety and privacy," sourced to "IBM AI Agent Security Best Practice." "Critical event" is never defined anywhere in the Research Strategy. No adverse-event taxonomy, no severity grading, no reporting timeline, no remediation protocol, and no independent safety monitoring structure is described for a system that will file benefits applications, contact government offices, and place calls on behalf of families. A single vendor's best-practice guidance is also a weak anchor relative to available frameworks (NIST AI RMF, OWASP LLM Top 10, HIPAA Security Rule).
- **Pricing methodology is a step backwards from evidence the applicant already possesses.** The Aim 3 price is to be set from stated willingness to pay collected from providers who received the product free — an instrument well known to be biased by hypothetical framing and reciprocity — despite the Progress Report documenting revealed willingness to pay at approximately $275 per placement from three of four providers who actually paid. Compounding this, Aim 3 tests a single price point across eight markets. Since revenue equals hires × price × markets, price is one of three multiplicands, and the CRP as designed will produce no demand curve and no elasticity estimate. Randomizing two or three price points across the eight Aim 3 markets would cost nothing and would substantially strengthen the resulting economics.
- **Recruitment feasibility is asserted rather than computed, for all three populations.** Families: 440 episodes across eight counties over nine months is approximately 6.1 enrolled longitudinal episodes per market-month, from a national organic base of ~15,500 monthly visitors; no traffic-to-assessment-to-consented-episode funnel is presented, despite the applicant having exactly this data. Workers: 150 placements across eight markets requires, at the gate's own 25% placement yield, roughly 600 qualified workers reaching provider handoff, which against the pilot's observed 900-applicant → 100-accepted → 25-placed funnel implies applicant volume several multiples above anything demonstrated, in eight counties where no university relationships are documented. Providers: 15 agencies absorbing 150 student hires is ten hires per agency in nine months, against a prior pilot that placed 25 workers in total. Each of these may be achievable; none is shown to be.
- **The timeline is serially compressed with no slack for outcome ascertainment.** Aim 1 (four engineering builds plus a 75-participant moderated study) occupies Year 1; Aim 2 accrues for nine months; Aim 3 accrues for nine months in two waves. Episodes initiated late in the Aim 2 window require follow-up to reach a terminal state — LTSS and Medicaid determinations routinely take months — yet no ascertainment period is budgeted between Aim 2 accrual and the Aim 2 analysis that must set Aim 3 pricing and configuration. The Commercialization Plan implicitly concedes the compression by modeling Year 3 revenue over "six paid-month equivalents" rather than nine, and Wave 2 of Aim 3 necessarily receives substantially less than nine months of exposure.
- **Inclusion is not addressed, and the recruitment design works against it.** The Research Strategy contains no discussion of inclusion by sex, race, ethnicity, or age, and no plan to ensure representation of the populations with the greatest unmet need. This is not curable by the Inclusion form alone, because the acquisition strategy — organic search plus paid social advertising to a self-service web and mobile portal — systematically selects for digitally connected, English-proficient, higher-health-literacy households. The application's own premise is that the families most at risk are those navigating Medicaid spend-down and public aid. A design that cannot reach them undermines both the scientific claim and the institutional value proposition, since MA and Medicaid managed-care buyers purchase for exactly the populations this channel under-recruits.
- No regulatory analysis is presented beyond the assertion that FDA approval is not required and a table cell citing "referral-compliance review" and "HIPAA-aligned data practices." The proposed activities carry real, state-variable regulatory exposure that is nowhere analyzed: employment-agency licensure and joint-employer liability in worker placement; state patient-brokering and anti-kickback exposure once institutional buyers pay for navigation that routes to providers; regulation of paid Medicaid application assistance; TCPA and two-party-consent obligations for agent-initiated calls and SMS; and business-associate status under HIPAA once contracting with MA plans and ACOs.
- Aim 3 does not test the institutional revenue engine in any form. Task 3.2 produces a specification for a post-CRP proof of concept and an evidence package built from Aim 2/3 descriptive results plus "published evidence and external economic modeling." No institutional buyer is engaged, no letter of intent is sought, no pilot is scoped with a named organization, and no willingness-to-pay signal from that segment is collected. The aim that is supposed to "produce commercialization evidence" produces it for only one of two revenue engines, and not the one that matters most.

## 5. Environment — Score: 4

*Assumption stated explicitly: subcontractor, consultant, and institutional letters were not supplied and are assumed adequate to substantiate the roles described. No score reduction is applied for their absence.*

### Strengths

- Substantial existing infrastructure rather than infrastructure to be built: a nationally deployed CareNavigator, an expert-curated database of more than 72,000 provider and aid-program records across all fifty states, 578 program guides, and organic distribution reaching families in nearly every county.
- Clemson University and Fan provide independent, established capability in epidemiology, mixed-methods evaluation, and health-services research, with IRB infrastructure and prior successful execution on Phase IIB. Placing the human-subjects work outside the company is the correct structural choice and strengthens the credibility of Aim 1 and Aim 2 evaluation.
- Demonstrated ability to publish: four peer-reviewed evaluations, two of which are cited (JMIR Aging 2024; JMIR Form Res 2026). For an SBIR company this is a meaningful indicator of research rigor and of the capacity to produce the kind of documentation institutional buyers eventually require.
- The company describes appropriate professional support functions — CPA/accounting, specialized legal and compliance counsel, HIPAA-aligned data practices, federal research and IRB administration experience — proportionate to a company of this size.
- Software-only production with no manufacturing, inventory, or physical distribution dependency, and a centralized market-activation model that does not require local offices, is a genuinely favorable operating structure for geographic replication.

### Weaknesses

- **The company's ability to sustain itself is entirely federally dependent, and the criterion asks directly about commercialization track record from prior SBIR awards.** SBIR/STTR funding is reported as 100% of revenue in each of the past five years, with prior-award commercialization revenue characterized as limited one-off pilots. On the evidence in these documents, the CRP is not accelerating a commercializing company; it is the company's operating budget.
- No prior institutional equity financing has been completed. The Commercialization Plan states this plainly, which is to its credit, but it means the applicant has never demonstrated the ability to raise the third-party capital the mechanism expects it to raise.
- Staffing is thin for the proposed environment. Two engineers (one added at award start) will build and maintain an agentic execution system with browser automation, telephony, fax, and document assembly while it operates live in sixteen markets. Two operations personnel will carry family and provider operations, escalation handling, and confirmation follow-up across approximately 880 longitudinal episodes and 150 worker placements. No dedicated data/compliance, customer success, or finance function exists, and the plan defers each until "workload and strategic importance justify" it.
- Regulatory capability is asserted at the level of a table entry. For a project whose activities include placing workers with licensed providers, preparing public-benefit applications, and preparing to contract with risk-bearing institutions, the absence of a named regulatory or compliance resource and any regulatory analysis is a genuine environmental gap, not a documentation gap.
- The plan's operational dependency on university relationships across sixteen new counties is described only as "existing and newly formed university relationships with pre-health advising offices" and "career centers and job boards; student organizations; advisors and faculty; career fairs." Per this review's standing assumption, whatever letters exist substantiate the relationships as described — but as described they are generic, and the Staffing throughput on which all CRP revenue depends requires that this channel be stood up rapidly in geographies where nothing is documented.

## CRP Progress Report

### Strengths

- The Progress Report is organized as an explicit risk-retirement ledger (Table 6) mapping each risk to the funding source that retired it and the specific evidence produced. This is the correct structure for this section and is executed better than most.
- Technical foundation is credibly retired: an expert-curated national database of more than 72,000 aid programs and providers across fifty states, 578 program guides, and an eldercare-tuned LLM.
- User acceptance is retired with peer-reviewed, quantitative evidence: usability 4.57/5; technology acceptance 5.83/7 after four weeks (n=65); multi-agent version 5.73/7 (n=31).
- Family demand is retired with operating data: organic traffic from roughly 50 visits per day in 2023 to more than 500 per day, from nearly every county, at near-zero acquisition cost.
- Provider participation is supported by 200+ I-Corps customer-discovery conversations and more than 700 providers claiming Olera profiles.
- Workforce reachability is supported by a real pilot: more than 900 undergraduate applicants and 25 placements into provider jobs.
- **Early willingness to pay is the report's most valuable single item.** Four providers trialed Caregiver Staffing, three paid at approximately $275 per placement, and used the service across multiple semesters. Revealed price plus repeat use, even at n=3, is stronger evidence than most CRP applications present. The explicit statement that delivery was deliberately manual in order to learn the workflow before automating it is exactly the right justification for what the CRP now proposes to build.
- The report is disciplined about what remains unretired and assigns each open risk to a specific aim.

### Weaknesses

- **After five years and ~$5.7M of federal support on a platform whose declared north star is established care, the Progress Report contains no measurement of care established.** No rate, no count, not even self-reported. This absence is the direct cause of the Approach's most serious defect: because no baseline exists, the Aim 2 gate of 35% is unanchored, and reviewers cannot tell whether the CRP is proposing to improve on 5% or on 45%.
- Commercial evidence is thin in absolute terms and the report does not contextualize it. Three paying providers and approximately 25 paid placements is, on the plan's own pricing, on the order of $7,000 in lifetime commercial revenue. The report reports the fact but not the scale, and a reviewer computing it will find the gap between "willingness to pay is de-risked" and the underlying magnitude uncomfortable.
- Engagement quality behind the headline reach numbers is not reported. Of 15,500 monthly visitors, how many complete a needs assessment? Of 700 claimed profiles, how many providers are active? Of 900 student applicants, how many completed screening, and over what period and geography? These are the conversion rates the Aim 2 recruitment plan depends on, the company has them, and their omission is conspicuous.
- The Research Strategy describes the multi-agent eldercare AI as "entering production," meaning the platform on which Aim 1 builds is not itself fully deployed. This is a material qualifier on the claim that the technical foundation is retired, and it is not discussed.
- The Research Strategy claims "Four peer-reviewed evaluations with family caregivers"; the Commercialization Plan cites two. The other two are neither cited nor described in either document.
- Third-party investment readiness — which this section is explicitly required to address — is addressed only prospectively. No investor conversation, term sheet, letter of intent, convertible instrument, accelerator investment, or non-federal capital of any kind is reported as having occurred, despite participation in Blackstone Techstars and NSF I-Corps.

## Commercialization Plan

*Assessment: **Moderate***

### Strengths

- The Statement of Need answers all four required questions directly and the Valley of Death is framed with an economically coherent argument: private investors must underwrite completion and deployment before institutional value is established, while institutional buyers require real-world evidence before purchasing — a genuine circularity that non-dilutive funding is well-suited to break.
- The plan explicitly considers and rejects the alternatives to federal funding on substantive grounds rather than dismissing them: charging families would create the greatest barrier for households already unable to afford care; charging providers for referrals would introduce steering incentives and exclude some federally reimbursed providers.
- **The financial model is arithmetically coherent and internally consistent**, which is uncommon. Every figure reconciles: 2 hires/market-month × $250 = $6,000/market-year; 8 paid markets × 6 paid-month equivalents = ~$24K in Year 3 with a ~$48K exit run rate; 15 markets = ~$90K in Year 4; 25 markets = ~$150K Staffing + ~$250K institutional = ~$400K in Year 5; 100/250/500 markets = ~$0.6M/$1.5M/$3.0M. The projection ladder holds together.
- **The plan separates published benchmarks from company assumptions from CRP hypotheses in an explicit table column (Table 7).** It repeatedly refuses to overclaim: $250 is labeled "an Aim 3 pricing hypothesis, not an asserted market price"; institutional value per relationship is labeled "Planning hypothesis, not a benchmark"; the base case assumes zero institutional revenue during the CRP and in post-CRP Year 4; and Table 9 states that "Revenue values are planning projections rather than CRP success criteria." This discipline is a real strength and should be preserved.
- The five-year commercialization timeline (Table 9) ties each period to a commercialization milestone and an explicit decision or financing gate, and states the no-go condition at each.
- Market-acceptance hurdles are tabulated against the specific CRP activity intended to retire each (Table 5), which is the right structure.
- The unit of commercialization is well-chosen and well-defined: a county-centered local market within which demand, participation, capacity, outcomes, acquisition cost, and commercial performance are attributed and measured. Replication of a validated local model without requiring national share is a credible growth mechanic.
- The IP section is honest about resting on trade secret, copyright, access control, and data accumulation rather than claiming a patent position it does not have, and it commits to an invention-disclosure review process during the award.

### Weaknesses

- **The near-term revenue engine is too small to carry the financing thesis.** At the plan's own base case and its own TAM of 1,233 counties, fully-penetrated Caregiver Staffing revenue is approximately $7.4M annually. The plan's own scenario table stops at $3.0M at 500 markets. A $3–5M institutional raise against a beachhead of this size, with the larger engine unvalidated, is a difficult financing story, and the plan does not address it.
- **The plan never reaches self-sustainability inside its own projections.** Year 4: $1.4M operating requirement against ~$90K revenue. Year 5: $1.85M against ~$400K. Cumulative two-year deficit is roughly $2.76M against a $3–5M raise, exiting Year 5 still burning ~$1.45M annually with revenue covering 22% of costs. Reaching breakeven on Staffing alone at $6,000 per market-year requires roughly 300 paid markets. No path to breakeven, and no Series-A-readiness metric other than "evidence," is presented.
- **The market-count error described under Significance sits in the section that sizes the opportunity**, and Section 4's "more than 3,100 such geographic units" conflicts with Section 8's 1,233-county TAM.
- **Institutional revenue — 62% of the Year 5 base case and effectively all long-run revenue — rests on a chain of unvalidated links**: that Aim 2/3 evidence will be persuasive to risk-bearing buyers; that a Year 4 proof of concept can be secured; that it will demonstrate utilization or cost reduction; and that this converts to a ~$250K annual relationship. No step in this chain is tested during the award, no institutional customer discovery is reported, and the applicant correctly labels the $250K figure a planning hypothesis — which is honest, and also means the largest revenue line in the model is unanchored.
- The revenue model counts hires rather than provider relationships, so it contains no retention, repeat-purchase, or lifetime-value analysis. Given a 75% annual caregiver turnover benchmark and pilot providers who returned across multiple semesters, recurring per-provider revenue is likely the strongest available commercial argument and the plan omits it entirely.
- Customer acquisition cost is committed to as a measurement in Aims 2 and 3 but is nowhere estimated in advance, so the plan presents revenue projections with no corresponding cost-to-acquire assumption. Contribution margin at $250 per hire cannot be assessed by a reviewer from what is provided.
- The IP position, while honestly described, is weak in absolute terms: no patents filed or issued are reported. The stated moat is a data asset whose accumulation threshold is never estimated, and the plan simultaneously proposes to expose "domain data and execution capabilities" to general-purpose AI interfaces — a distribution strategy that is defensible but that cuts directly against the data-and-distribution moat argued three sections earlier.
- Competition is enumerated but not analyzed. CareYaya is named as a direct competitor in the beachhead and receives no comparative treatment. General-purpose AI is named and its trajectory is not addressed.
- **Task numbering does not match the Research Strategy.** Section 7 states that "Task 3.4 converts those results of Aims 2 and 3 into a buyer-facing evidence package and post-CRP proof-of-concept specification." The Research Strategy contains only Tasks 3.1 and 3.2, and assigns that work to Task 3.2. There is no Task 3.4.

## Study Timeline

*Assessment: **Concern***

### Strengths

- The timeline is staged by risk exposure rather than by convenience, with gates that prevent progression to greater human or commercial exposure until prespecified criteria are met.
- The governing contingency rule — scope narrows before the calendar compresses — is stated explicitly and instantiated in three specific fallbacks.
- Aim 3 is structured in two waves of four markets, which creates a natural mid-course learning point.
- Independent workstreams are explicitly permitted to proceed in parallel where safe, which is realistic.

### Weaknesses

- No time is budgeted between Aim 2 accrual and the Aim 2 analysis for outcome ascertainment. Episodes enrolled in months 7–9 of a nine-month window cannot have reached a terminal care-establishment state by the end of that window, particularly for Medicaid and LTSS determinations that routinely take months. Either the effective accrual window is materially shorter than nine months or the analysis is performed on incompletely ascertained outcomes.
- Aim 2's results must set Aim 3's price, product configuration, automation envelope, and acquisition playbook. That analysis-and-reconfiguration interval is not shown on the timeline and appears to have no allocated duration.
- Aim 3's two-wave structure is inconsistent with the stated nine-month Aim 3 period: Wave 2 markets necessarily receive substantially less exposure, and the Commercialization Plan's modeling of Year 3 over "six paid-month equivalents" appears to acknowledge this without the Research Strategy doing so.
- Year 1 is heavily loaded: four substantial engineering builds plus IRB approval, protocol development, recruitment, and execution of a 75-participant moderated study across three user classes, with two engineers.
- Enrollment shortfall contingencies are addressed for progression (narrow scope) but not for accrual specifically. No plan to add markets, extend accrual, monitor accrual against a schedule, or adjust the analysis if 440 episodes are not reached is described.

## Human Subjects Design Visible in the Research Strategy

*Assessment: **Concern***

> **Note:** Full Human Subjects materials, Protection of Human Subjects, Inclusion Enrollment Reports, and the Clinical Trial forms were not supplied in this review package and were **assumed adequate**. They were not reviewed and no determination is made on them. Only issues intrinsic to the research design as described in the Research Strategy are assessed below — issues that no separate Human Subjects section could reasonably cure.

### Strengths

- The design places usability and trust testing on non-live, standardized simulated cases in a non-production staging environment before any real-world exposure, and gates Aim 2 on those results.
- The permission architecture is specified at the design level, not delegated: actions requiring attestation, legal authority, identity verification, or clinical judgment are bounded to remain human-controlled; agents are prohibited from enacting diagnosis, treatment, or interpretation of clinical information.
- The design explicitly distinguishes the person using the platform from the person whose information or benefits are involved, and bars agents from inferring legal decision-making authority from caregiver status alone. This is a sophisticated and correct safeguard that many agentic health designs miss.
- Escalation to human support for unsupported, stalled, or ambiguous workflows is built into the agent loop rather than added as a policy.
- Failure remediation is specified structurally: workflows can be narrowed, disabled, moved behind additional approval, or retired, and human-execution thresholds can be lowered.
- Licensed providers explicitly retain responsibility for interviewing, hiring, training, credentialing, supervision, and employment standards for placed workers, which is the correct allocation of liability and of participant protection.

### Weaknesses

- **"Critical event" is the gate for both Aim 1 and Aim 2 and is never defined.** No adverse-event taxonomy, severity grading, reporting timeline, remediation protocol, or independent monitoring structure is described, in a study in which software will submit public-benefit applications and contact government offices on behalf of older adults. This belongs in the research architecture, not in the Human Subjects form.
- **The consequences of agent error on real benefits applications are not addressed.** An incorrectly filed Medicaid or LTSS application can produce denial, delay, re-application, or eligibility consequences for a household at financial risk. No error-detection, correction, notification, or make-whole protocol is described.
- **The beneficiary is frequently not the consenting participant.** The platform user is typically a family caregiver; the person whose health, financial, and benefit information is processed and acted upon is an older adult who may have cognitive impairment. The design correctly separates their permissions but does not address the research-ethics implications of collecting and acting on the data of a non-consenting third party, nor capacity assessment, nor assent.
- **Agent-initiated third-party contact is unexamined.** Agents will email, SMS, fax, and place phone calls to providers and government offices. Disclosure of the automated nature of the contact, recording and two-party-consent obligations, and the treatment of information about third parties returned through those channels and persisted into the field-learning layer are not addressed.
- Aim 2 places newly recruited student workers into the homes of older adults. Provider responsibility for screening is correctly assigned, but the design does not discuss risk to the older adults served, worker safety in home settings, or whether adverse events in placements are captured by the study at all.
- No inclusion plan is visible. Because the recruitment channel (organic search plus paid social to a self-service portal) systematically under-reaches low-income, low-literacy, limited-English-proficiency, and less digitally connected households — the population with the greatest unmet need and the population institutional buyers would purchase for — this is a design-level rather than forms-level problem.

## Top Score-Driving Strengths

1. **Problem importance and quantification.** The unmet need is major, rising, well-sourced, and clearly connected to outcomes NIA cares about.
2. **The care-establishment endpoint.** Choosing confirmed care over referral or eligibility as the terminal outcome is a genuine conceptual advance over the field's standard and organizes the entire architecture coherently.
3. **Revealed provider willingness to pay.** Three of four pilot providers paid approximately $275 per placement and returned across multiple semesters. Actual money at an actual price is more than most CRP applicants can show.
4. **Team continuity and prior NIA execution.** The same PI and co-investigator who built and published on the platform across two prior awards, with a credible independent academic evaluation partner.
5. **Real, cheap distribution.** Organic growth from ~50 to >500 daily visitors nationally at near-zero acquisition cost is a material commercial asset.
6. **Intellectual honesty in the financial model.** Benchmarks, assumptions, and hypotheses are separated explicitly; the base case assumes no institutional revenue through post-CRP Year 4; revenue values are declared not to be success criteria. The arithmetic reconciles throughout.
7. **The field-learning architecture.** Provenance-gated, human-QA'd capture of local operational truth that does not exist on the public web is the most defensible long-run asset described in either document.
8. **Pre-specified gates with real stop conditions**, including explicit willingness to refine, pivot, or stop rather than scale an invalidated model.

## Top Score-Driving Weaknesses

1. **The primary endpoint cannot support the inference the commercialization thesis requires.** Single-arm, no comparator, no baseline, sponsor-defined and sponsor-adjudicated, with an unjustified threshold and near-zero design margin.
2. **No aim tests whether agent execution — the funded innovation — improves anything**, despite the platform being trivially able to contrast authorized against declined agent execution.
3. **The largest commercial assumption is untested by any aim.** Institutional buyers carry ~62% of Year 5 base-case revenue and essentially all long-term revenue; no discovery, engagement, letter of intent, or willingness-to-pay signal exists or is planned.
4. **The funded technology and the tested revenue engine are different products.** Nearly all Aim 1 engineering serves a product committed to remaining free; the monetized product does not require that engineering.
5. **The in-award revenue engine is small.** ~$7.4M fully-penetrated annual revenue at the applicant's own base case and TAM, against a $3–5M raise and a plan that never reaches breakeven within its own projections.
6. **Workforce throughput — on which all in-award revenue depends — is weakly supported**, requiring several multiples of the only demonstrated funnel across sixteen counties with generically described university relationships.
7. **Aim 1 verification is internal, subjective, and in places unfalsifiable**, and "critical event," the gate for both Aim 1 and Aim 2, is never defined.
8. **The Fundraising Plan does not demonstrate ability to raise third-party funds equal to or exceeding the federal request.** No prior institutional equity, no investor commitments, one quarterly advisor, and named funds described only as being in his network.
9. **No inclusion planning, with an acquisition channel that systematically under-reaches the highest-need population** — which is also the population institutional buyers would purchase for.
10. **Quantitative and cross-document errors in the sections that matter most** — a wrong TAM percentage in the section that sizes the market, and a reference to a nonexistent Task 3.4 in the section that describes how institutional evidence gets produced.

## Likely Discussion Dynamics

Assuming a competent Specific Aims page consistent with the Research Strategy, discussion would likely open on strong agreement about significance and about the applicant's prior NIA execution, then move quickly to the Approach.

**The dominant thread will be attribution.** Someone will ask what the care-establishment rate is today. The application cannot answer, and the panel will recognize that this makes the 35% gate unanchored. From there the discussion turns to the absence of any comparator and to the observation that the platform could randomize the agent-execution offer at essentially no cost. This is the argument most likely to move scores downward across multiple reviewers simultaneously, because it converts several separate criticisms — unjustified threshold, self-adjudicated endpoint, untested innovations, insufficient institutional evidence — into one root cause.

**The second thread will be the mismatch between funded technology and monetized product.** Expect a reviewer to observe that the CRP funds an agentic execution system for a product that will always be free, and monetizes a student-recruiting funnel that needs none of it. The applicant's counter — that capacity is a precondition for care establishment — is present in the documents and is genuinely responsive, but it does not fully answer why the CRP's engineering is on the free side.

**The third thread will be scale.** Once a commercially sophisticated reviewer computes $6,000 per market-year against 1,233 counties, the beachhead's ceiling becomes explicit, and the discussion will connect it to the Fundraising Plan's absence of investor evidence. This is where the "is this investable at award end?" question gets decided, and it is the second-most-likely score mover.

**Anticipated disagreement.** A minority will argue that CRP is explicitly a de-risking mechanism, that demanding a randomized comparator overreads a commercialization award, and that revealed provider payment plus an honest financial model plus real gates place this above the median. A commercially oriented reviewer may counter that CRP's purpose is precisely to produce evidence that unlocks private capital, and that evidence which will not persuade a payer has not served the mechanism's purpose. Expect a spread of roughly 3 to 6 with the mass at 4.

**Likely secondary discussion:** team capacity for a sixteen-market operation with two engineers and two operations staff; whether frontier general-purpose agents erode Innovation 2 within the award period; and the absence of any inclusion planning given the Medicaid-adjacent population the application says it serves.

## OVERALL IMPACT SCORE: 4

**Why this score.** This is a credible, well-organized CRP application from a continuous team addressing a problem of high public-health importance, with more real-world de-risking than most applications at this stage, a genuinely superior outcome definition, real distribution, revealed provider willingness to pay, and an unusually honest commercial model. It is very likely to produce a working system, an operationally informative pilot across sixteen counties, and a small but real staffing revenue line. What it is not likely to produce, as designed, is evidence that changes an institutional buyer's behavior or a commercial position that attracts capital substantially exceeding the federal request — because the primary endpoint is uncontrolled and self-adjudicated, the funded innovations are never contrasted against their absence, the institutional buyer is never engaged, and the only monetized product has a fully-penetrated base-case market of roughly $7.4M annually.

**Why not one point better (3).** A 3 would require that the CRP's evidence retire the assumptions the commercialization thesis depends on. It does not. Aim 2 measures a rate with no comparator, no baseline, no independent adjudication, and an unjustified threshold with essentially no design margin. Aim 3 tests neither the institutional revenue engine nor price elasticity — the two variables that dominate the revenue model. Aim 1's verification criteria are self-graded and, in one instance, unfalsifiable. The Fundraising Plan does not demonstrate the capability the mechanism specifically asks it to demonstrate.

**Why not one point worse (5).** A 5 would require that the commercial premise be speculative or the execution capability unproven. Neither holds. Providers have paid real money at a price consistent with the plan and returned across semesters; the workforce channel produced 900+ applicants; distribution is real and nearly free; the team has delivered on two prior NIA awards and published four peer-reviewed evaluations; the gates are pre-specified with genuine stop conditions; and the financial model is arithmetically sound and explicitly refuses to overclaim. The application's defects are concentrated in evidence design and commercial scale, both of which are addressable, rather than in premise or competence.

# FINAL APPLICANT DIAGNOSTIC

*Prepared after scores were locked. Ordered by expected score impact.*

## Must fix in the Research Strategy before submission

### RS-1. Add a comparator to Aim 2

- **Section:** Aim 2, Task 2.1; Aim 2 gate table.
- **Problem:** The primary outcome is single-arm with no comparator, no baseline, and no external benchmark. The application can report a rate but cannot attribute it.
- **Reviewer inference:** "They will measure something and will not be able to tell us what it means. Nothing downstream survives this."
- **Scoring consequence:** This is the largest single driver of the Approach score and it propagates into Significance and Overall Impact by undermining the institutional evidence claim.
- **Minimum change necessary:** Randomize the *offer* of agent execution within enrolled episodes (agent-execution offered vs. standard human-assisted navigation), or, at minimum, pre-specify a comparison between episodes in which families authorized agent execution and those in which they declined, with a stated adjustment strategy for confounding by indication. Two or three sentences plus a row in the gate table. This is the highest-leverage edit available anywhere in the application.

### RS-2. Report a baseline care-establishment rate and derive the gate from it

- **Section:** CRP Progress Report; Aim 2 gate table.
- **Problem:** The 35% threshold has no reference point, and the sample size appears reverse-engineered from it. At n=400, the gate passes only if the true rate is ≈40%+, an undisclosed design assumption.
- **Reviewer inference:** "The threshold was chosen to be passable, not to be meaningful."
- **Scoring consequence:** Approach; also weakens the Progress Report by highlighting that the north-star metric has never been measured.
- **Minimum change necessary:** Report whatever the company can measure today from existing users — even a retrospective self-reported rate from a sample of the existing base, explicitly labeled as preliminary — then state the threshold as an improvement over it, and add one sentence of precision justification for n=400 ("at an anticipated rate of X%, n=400 yields a 95% CI half-width of ±Y%").

### RS-3. Define "critical event" and specify safety monitoring

- **Section:** Aim 1 and Aim 2 gate tables; Task 1.2.
- **Problem:** "No unresolved critical event" gates two aims and is never defined. No adverse-event taxonomy, severity grading, reporting timeline, remediation protocol, or independent monitoring is described for software that will file public-benefit applications on behalf of older adults.
- **Reviewer inference:** "The safety gate is unfalsifiable, and errors with real financial consequences for vulnerable households have no defined handling."
- **Scoring consequence:** Approach and Human Subjects assessment. This is not curable by the Human Subjects form because the gate lives in the research design.
- **Minimum change necessary:** A short table defining event severity tiers with examples (e.g., incorrect submission requiring re-application; unauthorized action outside the permission envelope; disclosure of beneficiary information to an unintended party), the reporting timeline, the remediation and family-notification protocol, and who reviews. Reference a recognized framework (NIST AI RMF, OWASP LLM Top 10) alongside or in place of the single vendor guidance.

### RS-4. Replace self-graded Aim 1 criteria with measurable ones

- **Section:** Aim 1 gate table (agent workflow execution; field learning).
- **Problem:** "All expected actions per internal team assessment with no perceived errors" is unfalsifiable; "100% of new data acquired, normalized, and persisted" is not a credible criterion for an autonomous LLM pipeline.
- **Reviewer inference:** "They are grading their own exam, and one criterion cannot be failed."
- **Scoring consequence:** Approach; also weakens Innovation by making the technical claim untestable.
- **Minimum change necessary:** Specify the test-set size and case mix; state a numeric pass rate on workflow completion and permission enforcement; state precision and recall targets for field-learning normalization against a human-adjudicated gold standard; and name who performs the assessment, including at least one reviewer outside the build team.

### RS-5. Show the recruitment arithmetic for all three populations

- **Section:** Tasks 2.1 and 2.2.
- **Problem:** 440 episodes (≈6.1 per market-month) and 150 placements (requiring ≈600 qualified workers at the stated yield) are asserted without funnels, against a pilot that placed 25 workers in total.
- **Reviewer inference:** "They have this data and did not show it, which usually means it does not support the target."
- **Scoring consequence:** Approach and Study Timeline; it is also the feasibility question a skeptical reviewer raises first.
- **Minimum change necessary:** One short table per population: current observed conversion at each funnel stage from company data, the per-market monthly volume required, the implied top-of-funnel need, and the channel mix that produces it. Name the specific universities or systems in or near the candidate Aim 2 counties.

### RS-6. Close the automation-metric loophole

- **Section:** Aim 2 gate table; Timetable contingency rule.
- **Problem:** The ≥70% automation-share criterion has a sponsor-defined denominator ("approved," "eligible for automation") that the Aim 1 narrowing contingency directly improves.
- **Reviewer inference:** "Narrowing scope makes this metric better. It cannot be failed."
- **Scoring consequence:** Approach.
- **Minimum change necessary:** Pre-specify the full set of administrative action classes a care-establishment episode requires; commit to reporting automation share against that fixed denominator as well as against the approved-and-eligible subset; and add a minimum-breadth criterion (e.g., "no fewer than N of the M defined workflow classes enter Aim 2").

### RS-7. Add inclusion planning and a channel that can reach the target population

- **Section:** Tasks 2.1 and 2.2; market selection.
- **Problem:** No inclusion discussion, and an acquisition channel (organic search plus paid social to a self-service portal) that systematically under-reaches low-income, low-literacy, limited-English-proficiency, and less digitally connected households — the population the application says it serves and the one institutional buyers purchase for.
- **Reviewer inference:** "The families most likely to fall through the cracks are the ones this design cannot enroll."
- **Scoring consequence:** Approach; it also weakens the institutional value proposition, since MA and Medicaid managed-care populations are precisely those under-recruited.
- **Minimum change necessary:** State enrollment targets by race, ethnicity, sex, age, and payer source; add at least one non-digital or partner-mediated recruitment channel (Area Agencies on Aging, FQHCs, hospital discharge planners, faith and community organizations — several are already named in the Commercialization Plan's Table 6 but not in the Research Strategy); and add a language-access statement.

### RS-8. Budget outcome ascertainment into the timeline

- **Section:** Timetable; Aim 2 → Aim 3 transition.
- **Problem:** Episodes enrolled late in Aim 2 cannot reach a terminal state within the accrual window, and no interval is allocated for Aim 2 analysis to set Aim 3 pricing and configuration.
- **Reviewer inference:** "Either accrual is shorter than stated or the analysis runs on incomplete outcomes."
- **Scoring consequence:** Study Timeline; Approach.
- **Minimum change necessary:** State the accrual window and the ascertainment window separately (e.g., six months accrual plus three months ascertainment), define a maximum follow-up after which an episode is censored with a stated terminal classification, and show the analysis-and-reconfiguration interval explicitly on Figure 9.

Name the advisors behind the "our investor advisors agree" claim or remove the attribution. Convert the milestone list into a dated fundraising sequence with named target investor categories, materials, and a defined Series A thesis. Where a letter of support exists, make the Commercialization Plan's text describe a role the letter can actually substantiate rather than depending on it to establish capability.

### CP-5. Add price experimentation and provider lifetime value
